## The current role: where I work today and what I do at Fundación CTIC

<!-- seccion: el-rol-actual -->

Since March 2025 I have been working as an Analytics Professional at Fundación CTIC, an
institution in the healthcare sector. It is my current responsibility and the most demanding
context I have worked in when it comes to quality, security, privacy, traceability and
information governance. The reason is fundamental: in healthcare, data does not represent only
transactions or indicators; it describes people, care processes and institutional decisions that
demand an especially high level of responsibility.

My work brings together two complementary responsibilities. The first consists of developing
analytical capabilities in Power BI that make it possible to integrate information, model
administrative and clinical processes, structure reliable indicators and offer leaders a common
base for understanding institutional performance and supporting their decisions. The second, and
currently one of the most important of my career, consists of leading the institutional
artificial intelligence strategy, guiding the way the organization identifies, evaluates,
prioritizes, designs and governs its AI initiatives, with the implementation of its AI management
system.

I work in a direct line with the Director of Planning and with the sub-directors of technology,
information management and quality. I do not lead a team of direct reports: I lead complete
processes that bring together a large number of people —today, 20 leaders of 15 administrative
and clinical processes and some 75 users of the analytical solutions—. It is a different form of
leadership from the one I exercised at Cafam with a team of 20 people: there I directed people;
here I direct the way an institution measures, decides and adopts artificial intelligence.

## What this role brings together: data engineering, AI agents and enterprise architecture

<!-- seccion: los-tres-campos-que-reune-el-rol -->

This role brings together the three fields on which I have built my professional profile. Data
engineering and analytics provide the structure needed to represent institutional reality through
reliable information: at Fundación CTIC that translates into analysis and visualization models
for administrative and clinical leaders, into data cleaning, integration and standardization
processes, and into process-based dashboards for tracking key indicators. Artificial intelligence
applications and agents make it possible to turn that information and the organizational
knowledge into new capabilities for assistance and execution. Enterprise architecture finally
connects those solutions with the institution's processes, controls, responsibilities and
objectives.

The four functions my CV describes for this position are, in that order, analytics for
decision-making, data governance and quality, monitoring with business intelligence and continuous
improvement. The fifth, which did not fit in a bullet, is the one that today takes up most of my
judgment: the institutional AI strategy under UNE-ISO/IEC 42001:2025, with 23 instruments of the
management system in different states of progress.

## What I do not tell: patient data and confidentiality in healthcare

<!-- seccion: el-limite-de-confidencialidad -->

Everything I communicate about this experience keeps a limit I consider non-negotiable: I do not
expose patient data or clinical information; nor sensitive details of the processes, nor internal
knowledge whose disclosure could affect people or the institution. I can explain the
capabilities, the architecture principles, the governance practices and the way of working. I do
not reveal the information those capabilities operate on. That is why the figures in this
document —42 analytical products, 23 dashboards, 20 leaders, 15 processes, 12 AI opportunities—
are aggregated and do not name any process, any indicator or any person.

This reserve does not limit the solidity of the narrative. On the contrary, it demonstrates a
central principle of my work at Fundación CTIC: professional transparency does not consist of
disclosing information indiscriminately, but of explaining clearly the methods, the
responsibilities and the controls without compromising the confidentiality that makes trust
possible. It is the same principle I apply to the governance of personal data in healthcare: the
method is explained and what it protects is respected.

## Leading the institutional artificial intelligence strategy

<!-- seccion: estrategia-institucional-de-ia -->

Since March 2025 I have led the institutional artificial intelligence strategy with the purpose of
turning potentially isolated initiatives into a coherent, governable and sustainable
organizational capability. My responsibility does not consist only of identifying technological
opportunities, but of establishing a common way of deciding which initiatives should move
forward, under what conditions they can do so and how their behavior will be evaluated throughout
their entire lifecycle.

The strategy starts from a conviction: artificial intelligence should not be incorporated because
a technology is available, but because there is a relevant institutional need that can be
addressed responsibly and verifiably. Before proposing a solution, I need to understand the
problem, the people and processes involved, the available information, the expected result, the
risks introduced and the alternatives that could solve the need with less complexity. I do not
start from the available technology but from the problems, the decisions and the capabilities the
institution needs to strengthen.

With that rule, in little more than a year the strategy has made it possible to identify 12
artificial intelligence opportunities and formally evaluate 7 use cases. The number matters less
than the filter: each of those 12 opportunities first had to declare what problem it solved and
for whom.

## Not every problem needs AI: how I choose the solution pattern and build the portfolio

<!-- seccion: que-iniciativas-necesitan-ia -->

Not every problem needs artificial intelligence and not every AI initiative requires an agent.
Some needs can be addressed through a process improvement, an analytical product in Power BI, a
conventional automation or a rule-based application. Others may justify capabilities for
classification, prediction, generation, knowledge retrieval or tool coordination. My function
consists of selecting the appropriate solution pattern and preventing the technology from
becoming a default answer.

The strategy must also establish an institutional portfolio of initiatives. This involves
organizing the use cases according to their potential value, feasibility, availability of
information, level of risk, dependence on third parties, integration complexity and adoption
capacity. Prioritization does not seek to identify only the most innovative ideas, but those that
can produce learning and value without exceeding the institutional capacity to implement,
supervise and sustain them. Of the 12 opportunities identified, 7 were formally evaluated and 3
were prioritized: that proportion is the result of applying the filter, not of a lack of ideas.

Each initiative must have an explicit purpose, an institutional owner, a clearly identified
population or process, expected results and evaluation criteria defined before it begins. It must
also specify what information it will use, which decisions it will support, which actions it could
execute and which responsibilities will necessarily remain with people. Today 2 of the 3
prioritized initiatives have that file complete and documented.

## From demonstration to institutional capability: a common vision for AI

<!-- seccion: de-la-demostracion-a-la-capacidad -->

This way of working makes it possible to differentiate a technological demonstration from a
capability ready to be integrated into the institution. A demonstration proves that something can
be done. An institutional capability additionally demands reliability, security, integration,
adoption, supervision, documentation and mechanisms to respond when the result does not match
what was expected. It is the same distinction I learned at Vesting between an agent that works in
a test and an agent ready to operate, now applied to a health institution.

My responsibility is to connect these dimensions within a common vision. The artificial
intelligence strategy cannot move forward separately from the data strategy, the technological
architecture, security, risk management, knowledge of the processes and institutional policies.
Each initiative must be understood as part of an organizational system and not as an independent
product.

The strategy is in a progressive process of structuring and consolidation. This involves building
the institutional instruments —23 to date—, establishing responsibilities, evaluating the first
use cases and developing the capacity needed to govern the initiatives throughout their entire
lifecycle. My work therefore combines leadership of the strategic vision with the practical
construction of the conditions that will make it possible to implement it sustainably.

## The AI management system we are building under UNE-ISO/IEC 42001:2025

<!-- seccion: sistema-de-gestion-de-ia -->

The strategy is structured following the principles and requirements of ISO/IEC 42001. The
edition we base ourselves on is UNE-ISO/IEC 42001:2025, the Spanish adoption of the international
standard ISO/IEC 42001:2023. This means that artificial intelligence is not addressed only as a set
of technological projects, but as a capability that needs policies, objectives, responsibilities,
processes, controls, evaluation and continuous improvement.

In this work I clearly distinguish two sources of guidance that complement each other but are not
confused. The applicable legal obligations come from the Colombian legal system and from the
requirements that correspond to the institutional context. The standard, on the other hand, is
the reference with which we organize the management system: who answers for what, how the risks,
opportunities and impacts of each AI system are managed, and how it is demonstrated that the
system works. How this governance compares with that of banking and that of the startup is in
the data governance and AI governance document.

My previous experience with quality management systems gave me an especially valuable foundation
for taking on this challenge. From my first professional experience, at Inglopres in 2016, with
ISO 9001:2015, I learned that it is not enough to perform an activity correctly: it is necessary
to establish what doing it well means, who answers for it, what evidence makes it possible to
evaluate its compliance and how it can be improved systematically. ISO/IEC 42001 shares the same
high-level structure, and the implementation starts the same way: with the context, the
interested parties, the roles and the responsibilities. I am currently carrying that discipline
over to the institutional management of artificial intelligence.

Applying the standard begins with understanding the institutional context. Before defining
controls or procedures, it is necessary to recognize why the organization uses or could use
artificial intelligence, which interested parties may be affected, what objectives it needs to
achieve and what legal, institutional and ethical obligations must guide its decisions.

## Roles, risk management and impact assessment of AI systems

<!-- seccion: roles-riesgos-e-impacto -->

Starting from the context, the strategy seeks to establish a governance framework that defines
roles, responsibilities and authorities. Not every AI-related decision belongs to the technical
team. The business must answer for the purpose and the expected results. Data owners must protect
the quality and the conditions of use of the information. The security, risk, compliance and
technology functions must intervene according to the nature of each initiative. Top management
must provide guidance and institutional backing; in my case, that backing is discussed in a direct
line with the Planning Directorate and with the sub-directorates of technology, information
management and quality.

Risk management is part of the design from the start. Each use case must be analyzed considering
the consequences of incorrect, biased, outdated or out-of-context answers; the effects of an
unwanted action; dependence on vendors; exposure of information; lack of explainability; and the
potential impact on people, processes and the institution. That analysis is one of the matrices
that form part of the 23 instruments of the management system.

The impact of the systems must also be assessed —the AI system impact assessment the standard
asks for, the AIIA—. A solution can be technically functional and still transform the
distribution of responsibilities, affect certain groups, modify people's experience or introduce
a dependency that had not been foreseen. The impact assessment makes it possible to observe the
system beyond its technical performance and consider its effects within the environment in which
it will be used. In healthcare that view is not optional: the affected group may be a patient.

## Inventory of AI systems, lifecycle and the 23 instruments of the management system

<!-- seccion: inventario-y-ciclo-de-vida -->

An initial condition of this approach is having visibility over the artificial intelligence
capabilities the institution develops, acquires or uses. That is why the strategy contemplates the
progressive construction of an inventory of AI systems that makes it possible to identify each
system, its purpose, owner, vendor where applicable, information sources, users, level of
autonomy, state within the lifecycle and main risks. It is not possible to govern consistently
what the organization cannot identify and characterize.

The strategy also incorporates lifecycle management. An initiative is not governed merely because
it has passed an initial review. Data changes, models evolve, integrations are modified, users
find new uses and risks can transform after implementation. That is why solutions must keep
owners, evidence, follow-up mechanisms and criteria for being updated, restricted or withdrawn
when they stop fulfilling their purpose.

As part of structuring the management system, the work today comprises 23 institutional
instruments, among them policies, procedures, evaluation matrices, criteria for use cases and
follow-up mechanisms: 8 are finished and 15 are under construction. Their progress is measured by
level of definition, review, approval and application, not only by the existence of the
document.

My goal is not to declare that the institution already holds a certification or that the
management system is fully implemented, unless those milestones are reached and can be formally
demonstrated. My current responsibility consists of leading the structuring of the strategy and
helping to incorporate the principles and requirements of the standard into the way the
organization governs its artificial intelligence initiatives, with the rigor needed to be able to
demonstrate each advance.

## The ISO 42001 expert agent as support for the management system

<!-- seccion: el-agente-experto-en-iso-42001-en-ctic -->

To sustain the work on the 23 instruments I built the ISO 42001 expert agent I publish in the
showcase, one of the 13 agents of the portfolio. It answers with the standard in hand: every
normative claim cites its corpus entry with clause and page, or declares the gap, and stamps the
date on which it was verified to still be in force. Its corpus synthesizes the 38 controls of
Annex A and the 24 carrier documents an auditor asks for in the documentary phase of a
certification.

In practice, it helps me review each instrument against the requirements that apply to it and
avoid ruling from a model's memory: no ruling with consequences moves forward without a person's
approval. It does not replace an audit nor certify anyone; it locates requirements, orders
questions and makes visible what is missing. Its complete design —why an expert agent is not a
document dropped into a RAG— is in the data governance and AI governance document.

## From the strategy to a governed portfolio of AI initiatives

<!-- seccion: portafolio-gobernado -->

A strategy acquires value when it can be turned into concrete decisions. That is why one of my
main focuses consists of establishing an institutional process for receiving, analyzing and
prioritizing artificial intelligence proposals. The purpose is to prevent initiatives from moving
forward only out of technological enthusiasm, pressure from a vendor or the isolated interest of
an area.

Each proposal must clearly present the problem it seeks to solve, the users and processes
involved, the expected benefit, the information required and the way a satisfactory result will
be recognized. It must also identify initial risks, dependencies, owners, integration needs and
adoption conditions. It is the same file that 2 of the 3 prioritized initiatives have complete
today.

The evaluation must determine whether artificial intelligence is necessary and, if so, what type
of capability is appropriate. An initiative may require predictive analytics, language
processing, knowledge retrieval, content generation, support for a decision or controlled
execution of tasks. Correctly defining that need avoids building agents for problems that can be
solved with simpler, more predictable and more sustainable instruments —sometimes, with a Power
BI dashboard—.

## Progressive validation: 12 opportunities, 7 cases evaluated, 3 initiatives prioritized

<!-- seccion: validacion-progresiva -->

I also try to make validation progressive. When there is uncertainty, the initial goal should not
be to deploy a complete solution, but to test the most important hypotheses at the lowest
reasonable cost and risk. A data exploration, a controlled test or a limited prototype can
generate enough evidence to move forward, modify the approach or stop the initiative before
committing larger-scale resources.

Stopping or redesigning a use case after correctly invalidating a hypothesis does not represent a
failure. Avoiding an unfounded investment also generates value. Responsible management of
innovation requires recognizing when the evidence does not support an idea and preserving the
institutional capacity to change direction.

With this approach, the portfolio stops being a list of projects and becomes a governance
mechanism. It makes it possible to compare initiatives under common criteria, balance value and
risk, allocate resources consciously and build an implementation sequence consistent with the
organization's maturity.

To date, the strategy has made it possible to identify 12 artificial intelligence opportunities,
formally evaluate 7 use cases and prioritize 3 initiatives for validation or progressive
development. Of these, 2 have documented owners, expected results, initial risks and evaluation
criteria. That 5 of the 7 evaluated cases have not yet moved forward is the filter working, not
an inventory of failures.

## Process-based analytics, for two audiences: administrative and clinical leaders

<!-- seccion: dos-audiencias -->

I have developed analytical solutions in Power BI for administrative and clinical leaders,
structuring semantic models, indicators and visualization experiences adapted to their
responsibilities, but grounded in consistent institutional definitions. They are 42 analytical
products —among them 23 dashboards— that today are used by 20 leaders of 15 processes and some 75
users.

These audiences work on the same organization, but they do not necessarily ask the same questions
or use the information for the same purpose. Administrative management concentrates on
dimensions such as resources, capacity, timeliness, productivity and compliance. Clinical
management observes the continuity of the processes, the conditions of care and the results
associated with its responsibility: the quality indicators a healthcare provider (IPS) reports
and those that accreditation requires.

My work consists of building a common analytical language without erasing those differences. An
indicator must keep a single institutional definition, even though it can be presented with
different levels of detail and contexts according to the responsibility of whoever uses it. Top
management needs to understand trends, risks and response capacity. Process owners need to
identify constraints and deviations. Operational teams need timely signals to act.

## One indicator, one definition: semantic models that separate the metric from its presentation

<!-- seccion: un-indicador-una-definicion -->

This distinction between audiences has led me to go deeper into the design of semantic models in
Power BI that separate the definition of the metric from the way it is presented. The logic must
remain consistent, while the analytical experiences adapt to the questions, responsibilities and
decisions of each audience. It is the same modeling discipline that the DP-600 validates and that
I applied at Banco Pichincha and at Vesting, now with the additional demand that a badly defined
clinical indicator can be read against the care of a person.

I also try to carefully differentiate between facts, interpretations, estimates and
recommendations. In a highly sensitive context, communication must make it possible to recognize
what information was observed, what result was calculated and what conclusion comes from the
analysis. This clarity protects trust and prevents an inference from being interpreted as a
certainty.

My goal is not to get every person to look at exactly the same screen. It is to ensure that each
of the 20 people who lead the 15 processes can act from their responsibility on a coherent and
traceable institutional representation of reality.

## Data governance and quality in healthcare: personal data, habeas data and Law 1581

<!-- seccion: gobierno-y-calidad -->

I have managed data cleaning, integration and standardization processes aimed at strengthening
its quality, consistency and reliability, in alignment with institutional policies and needs.
This experience represents an evolution of the lessons previously learned in the financial sector,
at Banco Pichincha, and in the platform for AI agents at Vesting, now within an environment in
which the sensitivity of the information demands especially rigorous controls.

Data governance in healthcare has a layer that in banking and in the startup did not weigh the
same: the protection of personal data. The institution is subject to the habeas data regime and
to Law 1581, and that is why governance includes anonymization when an analysis does not need to
identify anyone, and the principle that a piece of personal data is used for the purpose for which
it was authorized, not for any purpose that can technically be accessed.

In this context, governance cannot be limited to documenting definitions or assigning
permissions. It must connect the provenance of the information, its purpose, the transformations
applied, the institutional responsibilities and the conditions under which it can legitimately be
used.

## The data quality rules: completeness, duplicates, reconciliation and thresholds

<!-- seccion: reglas-de-calidad -->

Quality must be evaluated in relation to use. A value can comply with its technical format and, at
the same time, not be sufficiently complete, timely or consistent to support a decision. That is
why the controls must be designed by understanding the process that produces the data and the
consequence a wrong interpretation could generate.

With that criterion I designed the quality rules that feed the 42 analytical products. I publish
them without figures, because the figures would describe internal processes, but I can say what
kind they are:

- completeness of the fields a decision needs, not of every field in the record;
- duplicates between records that represent the same person or the same event;
- reconciliation between the sources that should say the same thing and do not always do so;
- thresholds that trigger a review before a value reaches an indicator.

I also try to ensure that the analytical products keep traceability from the indicator to the
sources and rules that support it. This capability makes it possible to explain results,
investigate discrepancies and prevent trust from depending solely on the person who built the
dashboard or the model in Power BI.

Standardization plays a strategic function here. When different processes use incompatible
definitions, the organization can have multiple reports without reaching a shared understanding.
Establishing common structures, concepts and rules allows the conversation to advance from which
figure is correct toward which decision should be made.

## Governing data and governing artificial intelligence are the same responsibility

<!-- seccion: gobernar-datos-y-gobernar-ia -->

This data governance foundation is indispensable for artificial intelligence. An application or an
agent should not use information only because it can technically access it. There must be an
authorized purpose, a clear need, an identifiable responsibility and explicit conditions for its
use. Technological capability does not replace the legitimacy of the use, and in healthcare that
legitimacy runs through habeas data.

In healthcare, governing data and governing artificial intelligence are closely connected
responsibilities. The reliability of intelligent solutions depends on the quality, provenance,
context and authorization of the information they use. That is why the AI strategy of Fundación
CTIC cannot be built as a layer independent of the institutional data architecture and
governance: the 12 opportunities identified were evaluated, among other criteria, by the
availability and the conditions of use of the information they would need.

## Analytical solutions and process-based dashboards in Power BI

<!-- seccion: tableros-por-procesos -->

I have developed analytical solutions in Power BI for tracking administrative and clinical
processes, integrating information, semantic models, indicators and visualization experiences
oriented to the needs of leaders with different responsibilities within the institution: 42
products, of which 23 are dashboards.

Power BI does not work in this context only as a tool for building dashboards. It is the
analytical layer through which data is organized, acquires meaning and becomes usable
information for institutional monitoring. Behind each visualization there is a job of
preparation in Power Query, integration, modeling, definition of measures in DAX, validation and
access control that determines whether the result can be used with confidence.

Organizing the dashboards by process and not only by area is a deliberate decision that comes
from my training in Industrial Engineering. An area can show favorable indicators while the
process that runs through it accumulates waits, handoffs, rework or constraints that no unit
observes completely. The process perspective makes it possible to analyze the end-to-end journey
and understand how administrative and clinical responsibilities interact.

That is why I try to have the semantic models represent the entities, relationships and events
that explain how the process works, instead of only reproducing the structure of the sources or
the functional distribution of the institution. The purpose is for the metrics to keep a
consistent definition and be analyzable from different perspectives without fragmenting their
meaning.

## Result and intermediate indicators: the design of a dashboard begins with the decision

<!-- seccion: indicadores-a-lo-largo-del-flujo -->

Indicators work as gauges distributed along the flow. Result indicators show what finally
happened. Intermediate indicators make it possible to explain how that result was produced, where
a deviation appears and which part of the process requires deeper analysis. This combination
allows Power BI to go beyond the retrospective report and become an instrument for understanding
operational behavior.

The design begins with the decision and not with the visualization. Before building a dashboard, I
try to establish who will use the information, what question they need to answer, what level of
detail they require, how often it must be updated and what conditions must be made visible. This
orientation avoids developing extensive products that show large amounts of information but do
not help understand what deserves attention.

I also differentiate the needs of each audience without modifying the institutional definition of
the metrics. Top management needs to observe trends, risks and response capacity. Process owners
need to identify constraints, deviations and causal relationships. Operational teams need
information detailed enough to understand concrete situations. The semantic model must keep a
single logic, while the Power BI experience adapts its depth and presentation to each
responsibility: it is what allows 42 products to serve 20 leaders and some 75 users without there
being 42 different definitions of the same indicator.

## Improvement plans: what I designed, what I implemented and the close to 60% less effort

<!-- seccion: planes-de-mejora -->

My contribution does not consist of claiming the implementation of the improvement plans that
belong to the institutional owners. From the analyses, I designed improvement plans for the
process owners, who are the ones who execute them. My responsibility is to build the analytical
capacity that makes it possible to identify opportunities, support conversations, prioritize
situations and follow up on the indicators related to the decisions adopted.

This distinction is important: analytics does not replace the responsibility of the process.
Power BI provides a reliable and understandable representation of reality, but it is the leaders
and institutional owners who determine the actions, allocate resources and manage their
execution. My function consists of ensuring that those decisions can rest on consistent,
traceable information that is fit for its purpose.

What I did implement, and a lot of it, were improvements in my own process. The preparation and
consolidation of information that used to be done by hand was reduced by close to 60% of the
effort —a figure estimated on my own work, not an institutional measurement—, and the 10
analysis plans the processes follow today come out of the same governed base instead of being
rebuilt each time.

When the owners adopt a decision, the analytical solutions contribute to tracking its results
through previously defined indicators. This closes the cycle between observation, decision and
evaluation without attributing to the platform, or to the analytics team, responsibilities that
belong to the governance and management of the process. Currently, the 42 analytical products are
in institutional use or follow-up, supporting 20 leaders of 15 administrative and clinical
processes, and have supported 10 improvement plans or decisions during the period evaluated.

## An institutional architecture for artificial intelligence: reuse without fragmenting

<!-- seccion: arquitectura-institucional-de-ia -->

Leading the artificial intelligence strategy also means defining how the initiatives will be able
to coexist, reuse capabilities and evolve without creating a new technological fragmentation.
Each isolated project can solve a specific need, but a succession of disconnected solutions
increases costs, repeats mistakes and makes governance harder. With 3 prioritized initiatives and
12 opportunities in the portfolio, the question is no longer only whether each one works, but
what they share.

The institutional architecture must connect the technological capabilities with the decision and
governance processes. This includes determining which components can be shared, what information
is authorized for each purpose, which integrations should be centralized, how the solutions will
be evaluated and what mechanisms will make it possible to supervise them during their operation.
My goal is not only to define how a solution is built, but to establish the conditions under
which different solutions can coexist, reuse capabilities and evolve without fragmenting the
institution.

The architecture must preserve institutional memory. The lessons of one initiative must be
transformed into patterns, criteria, components and controls that can be used by the following
ones. The goal is not to impose a single solution for every problem, but to prevent each project
from solving integration, security, evaluation, traceability and supervision from zero all over
again. It is the lesson the 11-stage core process at Vesting left me: a replicable process is
worth more than any individual agent.

It must also design autonomy gradually. A solution can begin by providing information, advance
toward generating recommendations and execute certain tasks only when there is sufficient
evidence about its behavior and controls proportional to the risk. Maturity is not measured by
how many decisions can eliminate human intervention, but by the precision with which
responsibility is distributed between people and systems.

## Evaluating vendors and third-party solutions: dependence, exit and control

<!-- seccion: gobernar-proveedores-y-terceros -->

An institutional AI architecture must also recognize the role of third parties. Solutions provided
by vendors also need evaluation, conditions of use, responsibilities, integration, follow-up and
exit mechanisms. Outsourcing the technology does not automatically transfer the institutional
responsibility for its effects. At Fundación CTIC, dependence on third parties is one of the
criteria with which each of the 12 opportunities is prioritized.

The evaluation of third parties should not be limited to checking features, price or ease of
integration. It must also make it possible to understand what information the solution will use,
where it will be processed, what dependencies it introduces, what evidence it offers about its
behavior, how it manages updates and what responsibilities the vendor retains in the face of
failures, incidents or relevant changes. In healthcare, "where the information will be processed"
is not a technical detail: it is a personal data question.

I also consider it necessary to evaluate the institutional capacity to retain control over the
solution. This includes the conditions for supervising it, restricting it, replacing it or
withdrawing it if it stops responding to the authorized purpose. A technological dependency
becomes critical when the organization cannot explain the behavior of the capability, recover its
information or continue the process without the vendor.

My purpose is for the organization to be able to innovate without turning each initiative into an
exception. The architecture must allow experimentation to move forward, but also allow whatever
proves its value to be integrated, operated, supervised and evolved with consistent standards.

## What this role brings together: analytics, agents, architecture and management system

<!-- seccion: lo-que-reune -->

Analytics continues to be indispensable because it makes it possible to observe reality and build
trust in the data. Applications and agents extend the capacity to use knowledge and execute
certain tasks. Enterprise architecture connects these solutions with processes, security,
governance and strategy. ISO/IEC 42001 provides the management framework that makes it possible
to organize these capabilities around responsibilities, risks, evaluation and continuous
improvement.

This role represents the most advanced point of my professional evolution. I began by trying to
understand and improve processes. Then I built the data needed to measure them, the platforms to
analyze them and the models to anticipate certain behaviors. Power BI allowed me to turn that
architecture of data and semantic models into reliable decision experiences. Later I designed the
infrastructure needed to observe AI agents. Today, since March 2025, I lead the strategy with
which an institution can decide which intelligent capabilities it needs, under what conditions it
can use them and how it should govern them throughout their lifecycle.

My purpose is not to get the organization to use more artificial intelligence. It is to help it
use the right artificial intelligence for relevant problems, on authorized and reliable
information, within explicit limits and with sufficient evidence to evaluate its behavior and its
value. Analytics makes it possible to understand reality; Power BI turns it into an accessible
experience for decision-making; intelligent solutions extend the capacity to act; and the
management system ensures that this evolution happens with responsibility, traceability and
purpose.
