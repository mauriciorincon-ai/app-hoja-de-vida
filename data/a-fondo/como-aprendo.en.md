## Why this document exists: learning fast, with dates

<!-- seccion: por-que-existe -->

A CV describes what a person knows and has done up to a given moment. In fields such as data and
artificial intelligence, that snapshot loses currency quickly: platforms evolve, models change, new
architecture patterns appear, and capabilities that are advanced today can become ordinary
components tomorrow. Microsoft Fabric, the platform I got certified on in December 2024, was not
generally available when I started working at Vesting.

That is why my professional value cannot depend solely on a list of tools. It must also demonstrate
my capacity to identify relevant knowledge, absorb it in depth and turn it into solutions that work
under real conditions.

This chapter exists to make that capacity visible. It does not intend to claim generically that I
learn fast: it shows the dates. It explains how I select a new technology, how I organize the
knowledge, how I put it to the test in practice, what evidence I consider sufficient and how I turn
what I have learned into components, documentation and processes that other people can use.

Learning speed does not mean skimming a large amount of content either. Learning fast consists of
reaching an applicable understanding sooner, recognizing which principles remain, identifying which
particularities need experimentation and progressively reducing dependence on step-by-step
instructions. The DP-600 in five months, on a platform with less than a year on the market, is the
measure that best illustrates it.

## Learning as a process: the structure of Industrial Engineering and the usage dimension of Industrial Design

<!-- seccion: aprender-como-un-proceso -->

My way of learning is guided by verifiable results. A study track must end in a credential, an
application, an architecture, a research piece, an improvement or an asset that can be examined. If
the knowledge does not change my capacity to solve a problem, I do not yet consider the learning
finished. Thus, the Fabric track ended in the DP-600 and in a platform in operation; the Next.js
one, in this site with its CI and its tests.

Industrial Engineering provides the structure of this method. I treat learning as a process with a
need, inputs, a sequence of work, controls, results and feedback: the same lens with which I do a
time study or draw a process in BPMN. Industrial Design provides the usage dimension: knowledge
gains more value when it can become a solution, an experience or a tool that other people can
understand.

This document therefore demonstrates something more important than the number of technologies I know
today. It demonstrates that I have a system for absorbing the ones I will need tomorrow.

## How I decide what deserves to be learned: the concrete need first

<!-- seccion: como-decido-que-aprender -->

I do not try to follow every tool, model or framework that appears. In a field that evolves so
quickly, learning without a selection criterion can produce a lot of activity and little depth. That
is why, before starting a new track, I evaluate whether the capability solves a real problem,
strengthens my professional architecture or broadens the kind of responsibility I can take on. They
are four industrial-engineering criteria —resources are limited and are allocated by value,
constraint and result—: concrete need, permanence, evidence and opportunity cost.

The first is the concrete need. I prioritize what connects with a problem I already have in hand.
The DP-600 accompanied the construction of a data platform in Microsoft Fabric. The AI-103 relates
to my work on applications and agents. The AI-300 goes deeper into the evaluation, observability and
operation of intelligent solutions. Google Cloud now enters exploration because it would extend
architecture capabilities I have already developed on Microsoft toward a second ecosystem; I do not
declare a horizon or real use of BigQuery or Vertex AI as long as no built piece exists.

## Permanence, evidence and opportunity cost: why I also choose what not to study

<!-- seccion: permanencia-evidencia-y-costo -->

The second criterion is the permanence of the learning. A tool can change or disappear —the AI-102
and the DP-100 disappeared as certifications while I was studying—, but the principles of
architecture, modeling, evaluation, security and experience tend to keep their value for longer. I
prefer to invest in capabilities that allow me to understand several technologies rather than depend
exclusively on procedures tied to a specific version.

The third is the possibility of producing evidence. A track deserves priority when it can end in an
application, an architecture, a certification, a research piece or a reusable component. If I cannot
establish which result will demonstrate that I really learned, the initiative is not yet
sufficiently defined.

The fourth is opportunity cost. Every new capability competes with products, research and
responsibilities that are already under way: 32 public pieces to maintain and an institutional AI
strategy to lead. That is why learning must respond to an explicit priority and not only to
technological novelty. Choosing what not to study at a given moment is part of maintaining depth and
coherence.

Industrial Engineering provides this prioritization criterion: resources are limited and must be
allocated according to value, constraint and expected result. Industrial Design preserves the
relationship with the person and the real problem. A technology deserves to be incorporated when it
significantly expands what I can build for others, not merely when it lengthens my list of
knowledge.

studying a platform and formalizing what is already being applied.

## How I learn, concretely: a small problem, official sources and documentation from day one

<!-- seccion: como-aprendo-en-concreto -->

My method begins by selecting a problem that is small, real and demanding enough to force me to use
the new capability in depth. I do not initially seek to reproduce every possibility of a platform. I
look for a situation I cannot solve correctly without understanding its essential concepts. With
Fabric, in 2024, that problem was monitoring a single agent; the rest came afterwards.

The problem must produce a result that can be judged. A successful connection, a convincing screen
or a generated answer are not sufficient evidence. I need to establish what the solution must do,
which conditions it must meet, which risks it must control and how I will recognize that the
learning became a working capability.

Then I turn to the official sources. Primary documentation can be slower at the beginning, but it
reduces dependence on outdated explanations, simplifications and examples that only work under
specific conditions. It also makes it possible to understand the concepts in the same language the
platform uses and to detect changes when the product evolves.

I do not dismiss tutorials, articles or communities, but I use them as support and not as the final
authority. When two explanations produce different interpretations, I go back to the official
source, reproduce the behavior and document the conclusion I can sustain.

From the first day I record decisions, assumptions, tests and problems found. What is not documented
needs to be rediscovered. What is left structured can become a guide, a component, a template, a
test or a reusable rule.

Learning advances through short cycles. I build a limited capability, observe its behavior, identify
the next gap and extend the solution. This sequence reduces the chance of spending weeks on an
extensive architecture before checking its main assumptions. This site was built that way: 8
sprints, each with its plan, its log and its summary.

## Feedback, technical critique and correcting course: familiarity is not mastery

<!-- seccion: retroalimentacion-y-revision -->

Feedback introduces a source of evidence I cannot produce from development alone. A solution can
respond correctly to my tests and still be confusing, hard to adopt or insufficient for the person
who has to use it: I learned it with the Power BI dashboards at Banco Pichincha, where adoption by
more than 50 users was earned by listening, not only by modeling. That is why I observe users'
questions, mistakes, journeys and expectations as part of the learning process.

I do not incorporate every suggestion automatically. First I need to understand which problem it
reveals, whether it matches the purpose of the product and whether it represents an individual
situation or a broader pattern. Feedback gains value when it can be translated into a hypothesis, a
verifiable change and a result that can later be evaluated.

I also use technical review and well-founded critique to identify assumptions that might remain
invisible within my own reasoning. Exposing an architecture, a model or a method makes it possible
to distinguish between a decision that is truly solid and one that only seemed obvious because
nobody had questioned it.

Learning does not consist only of absorbing new knowledge. It also requires correcting
interpretations, withdrawing decisions that stopped being adequate and recognizing when the evidence
forces a change of approach. The capacity to revise a position is as important as the speed to build
the first version.

I also distinguish between familiarity and mastery. I can understand the function of a technology
after studying it and experimenting with it, but I only declare a demonstrated capability when I
have used it to produce a finished, verifiable and sufficiently documented result. That is why
Kubernetes appears in my profile as study and not as operation: I have not taken containers to
production.

My goal is not to memorize every option of a platform. I seek to understand its principles,
recognize its limits, know how to locate the right information and develop judgment to select an
alternative according to the problem. Learning this way can be more demanding during the first days
because it forces understanding before copying. However, it speeds up later decisions, reduces
dependence on recipes and produces knowledge that remains even when the tool changes.

## Documentation as the memory of learning: ADRs, logs and cycle summaries

<!-- seccion: documentacion-del-aprendizaje -->

Documenting is not an activity that comes after learning. It is one of the mechanisms through which
I learn. Writing a decision forces me to state which problem it solved, which alternatives I
considered, which evidence I used and under which conditions it could stop being valid. In the
applications that takes the form of architecture decision records, the ADRs: 14 in Hablemos San, 13
in Dash Agent AI, 8 in Velo.

A note that merely records a command or a sequence of steps has operational usefulness, but it does
not necessarily preserve the reasoning. I try to document the purpose, the assumptions and the
consequences as well, because that information makes it possible to adapt the knowledge when the
context changes.

Documentation turns an individual experience into reusable memory. A test avoids rediscovering a
failure. A template preserves a validated structure. A guide organizes the sources. A cycle summary
lets the next piece of work start from the real state and not from a later reconstruction: every
sprint of this site closes with a log and a summary that the next one reads before starting.

I also keep a clear distinction between different states of knowledge. A hypothesis is not a
finding. An exploration is not a consolidated capability. A practice recommended by a source is not
automatically an adequate decision for my architecture. Documenting the state prevents an initial
idea from gaining authority merely because it stayed in the repository long enough.

This discipline comes from my experience with management and quality systems, since ISO 9001:2015 at
Inglopres. A process does not become repeatable because one person remembers how to execute it. It
needs instructions, criteria, controls and evidence that make it possible to understand and improve
it.

In ARKHÉ, my agentic ecosystem, documentation also works as controlled context. Agents should not
reconstruct previous decisions from scattered fragments or assume why an architecture was designed a
certain way. Structured memory reduces ambiguity, avoids repeating discussions and lowers
unnecessary context consumption.

## Code first and justified AI, and a control is demonstrated by failing: how I apply the two pipeline rules when learning

<!-- seccion: las-dos-reglas-del-pipeline -->

Two rules govern what I build and live, in full, in the applications pipeline document: code first,
and generative artificial intelligence only where a deterministic solution does not suffice; and no
control counts until it has been seen going red. What interests me here is what those two rules do
to my way of learning.

The first forces me to understand the problem before delegating it. Trying to solve a part with code
avoids using the model as a substitute for an incomplete specification and makes it possible to
separate what needs creativity or interpretation from what must always behave the same way. When I
incorporate artificial intelligence, I do it within a hybrid architecture: the code manages
validations, contracts, transformations and limits; the model intervenes where its generative
capacity produces necessary value; the sources provide evidence; the controls verify what can be
checked. Every generative call must have a function, a delimited input and a sufficient reason to
exist. The AI-103 expands my capacity to build applications and agents under this logic; the AI-300
strengthens their evaluation and operation once they start being used in a sustained way.

The second is a way of studying. I do not consider a platform's security, recovery or
fault-tolerance mechanism understood until I have observed what happens when it stops working. The
happy path shows that a capability exists. The failure shows whether I really know how to operate
it. It applies equally to data, software and artificial intelligence: a data validation has to
detect its inconsistency, and an agent has to be evaluated against insufficient information, missing
sources, unavailable tools and instructions that try to take it outside its function. The AI-300 is
especially relevant here, because operating artificial intelligence requires controls that detect
degradation, behavior changes, integration errors and results that no longer meet the defined
criteria.

Learning gains more depth when it includes the conditions of failure. Understanding how a technology
works is important. Understanding how it fails, how that is detected and how it recovers is what
makes it possible to take responsibility for it.

## The AI-103 Super Guide turns the method into a tool

<!-- seccion: la-guia-del-ai-103 -->

When the AI-103 track became the current path to go deeper into artificial intelligence applications
and agents on Azure, I did not want to depend solely on a sequence of courses selected by third
parties. I built an agent capable of structuring and verifying a study guide from official sources:
the AI-103 Super Guide, one of the 13 agents published in the showcase.

The tool applies the same discipline I use to learn. Study is inverted: first a real project is
defined in Microsoft Foundry, and no track is considered complete until a coverage map demonstrates
how its contents relate to the official domains and to the proportion each one represents within the
assessment.

Every pedagogical statement needs an identifiable source and a verification date: every note carries
its source, its URL and its capture date, and a live ingestion warns which guide has expired when
the documentation changes. This condition is especially important in technologies that evolve
quickly, because a technically correct explanation can become outdated when the services, the names,
the interfaces or the assessed capabilities change.

The agent does not freely decide what a person should study from the model's memory. It retrieves
documentation, organizes contents, identifies gaps and preserves evidence about the provenance of
every recommendation.

The guide also distinguishes between nominal coverage and sufficient depth. Including a link for
every topic does not demonstrate that the track really prepares the student. It is necessary to
evaluate whether there are contents, exercises and verifications proportional to the importance and
difficulty of the domain: every guide closes with a checkpoint, and the result reorders the track
toward where I am weak.

This system turns certification preparation into a knowledge-architecture problem. There are
sources, entities, dependencies, weights, currency states, gaps and criteria for done —29 binary
acceptance criteria on its sheet—. The tool organizes those relationships and makes visible what is
still missing.

The project also demonstrates an important principle of my agentic ecosystem: agents must produce
verifiable deliverables and not only answers that are useful in the moment. The guide can be
reviewed, updated and used by other people because its value remains after the conversation ends. It
is my learning method transformed into a public piece: official sources, measurable coverage,
declared gaps, controlled updating and a result that another person can examine and use.

## What I do when the role asks for something I have never used: the capability map and Google Cloud in exploration

<!-- seccion: incorporar-una-plataforma -->

When I need to take on a new platform, I begin by identifying the capabilities I already know in
functional terms and the particularities I must learn within the new ecosystem.

I do not try to superficially translate the names of the services. A warehouse, a lake, an
orchestrator, an agent platform or an identity system can respond to comparable principles, but each
provider implements those responsibilities through different operating models, costs, limits and
tools.

First I build a capability map. I identify how the platform solves storage, integration,
transformation, identity, observability, deployment, security and governance. Then I select a small
problem that forces connecting the essential capabilities within a complete flow. That is how I
entered Microsoft Fabric in August 2023: with the map first and a single agent to monitor
afterwards.

The piece must be limited enough to be finished and demanding enough to prevent the learning from
being reduced to following a tutorial. It must include at least one architecture decision, one
integration, one control, one deliberate failure and one verifiable result.

Google Cloud currently represents the next exercise of this method, and it is in exploration. I do
not need to present the platform as a shortcoming or as a list of services I do not yet master. I
have turned it into a construction priority to extend my depth toward a second ecosystem through a
solution that connects BigQuery, generative artificial intelligence, agents, observability, identity
and cost control. As long as that piece does not exist, I do not claim real use of BigQuery or
Vertex AI, nor do I declare a horizon; the detail of that expansion is in the platform and
deployment document.

The ease with which I understand new architectures comes from working first on principles: data,
flows, constraints, interfaces, controls and results. Industrial Engineering provides that
functional structure. Technical experience then makes it possible to translate it into the
platform's concrete services.

My criterion for declaring a new capability incorporated is clear. Completing a course or running a
demo is not enough. I must be able to explain the architecture, deploy the solution, reproduce it,
observe its behavior, recognize its limits and leave evidence that another person can examine.

## How I know I really learned: six criteria

<!-- seccion: criterios-de-aprendizaje -->

I consider a capability learned when I can use it without depending on a memorized sequence and when
I can explain why the solution works, under which conditions it can fail and which alternatives
existed. There are 6 criteria, and a capability is not learned until it meets all six.

The first criterion is functional. The piece must solve the problem it was built for and pass
representative scenarios, not only the happy path used during development.

The second criterion is architectural. I must be able to justify the function of each component, its
dependencies and the reason it was selected. A solution that works but that I cannot explain or
modify safely does not yet represent sufficient mastery.

The third criterion is operational. The capability must be deployable, observable and maintainable.
I need to know what happens when a dependency fails, an input changes, the volume grows or an
unforeseen condition appears.

The fourth criterion is documentary. The necessary decisions, configurations and tests must be
preserved so that the learning does not depend exclusively on my memory.

The fifth criterion is transfer. Knowledge reaches a higher level when I can turn it into a guide,
component, template, practice or explanation that another person can use.

The sixth criterion is limits. I must be able to state precisely what I know how to do, what was
tested, what depends on certain conditions and what still remains in exploration. Acknowledging the
limit does not reduce competence; it demonstrates control over its scope.

These criteria avoid confusing exposure with mastery. Having read about a technology demonstrates
interest. Having taken a course demonstrates preparation. Having built a solution demonstrates
application. Being able to operate it, explain it and transfer it demonstrates a professional
capability. Microsoft Fabric met all six at Vesting: the platform worked, I can explain it, it
operated in real time, it was documented together with the core process, and I know how to say what
I did not do with it.

## What this means for an organization that hires me

<!-- seccion: que-significa-para-quien-contrata -->

For an organization, this method means that my capacity is not limited to the inventory of
technologies I have already used. I can take on a new platform, framework or pattern through a
structured process that reduces uncertainty and produces evidence from the earliest stages: the same
one with which I went from getting to know Microsoft Fabric in August 2023 to getting certified in
it in December 2024 with a platform already in operation.

I do not present every gap as an open promise of learning. I turn it into a delimited problem, an
authoritative source, a small architecture, a set of controls and a finished artifact. This makes it
possible to observe progress and evaluate the capability through results, not only through personal
confidence.

Nor is my speed based on improvisation. It arises from reusing principles, documentation, patterns,
components and criteria that have already been applied in other contexts. Every new piece of
learning starts from an accumulated professional architecture and not from a blank page.

This way of working reduces the risk of adopting technology by trend. Before building, I analyze
what value it adds, what responsibility it introduces, what it will cost and how it will integrate
with the existing capabilities. The organization gets learning and judgment, not just an
implementation.

I also bring transparency about the state of the capability. I distinguish what I master, what I
have applied, what I am formalizing and what remains in exploration. This precision makes it easier
to assign responsibilities, define support and avoid expectations based on ambiguous descriptions.

When a role demands a technology that is new to me, I do not ask the organization to ignore that
difference. I demonstrate that I have a proven method, with dates, to quickly transform it into an
applicable, documented and verifiable capability.

What an organization takes on when hiring me is not only the knowledge I have today. It also takes
on a system to learn, validate and operationalize what its next stage will demand.

## What remains when the tool changes

<!-- seccion: lo-que-permanece -->

Tools evolve quickly, but the principles that make it possible to use them responsibly remain for
much longer. Between Excel and VBA at Inglopres and Microsoft Fabric at Vesting lie seven years and
several generations of tools; what I did with them is the same: representing a process with reliable
data in order to decide better.

The need to understand the problem before building remains. The importance of preserving the
provenance of the data remains. The relationship between authority and responsibility remains. The
need to test, observe and document remains. The obligation to design for the people who will use or
be affected by the solution remains.

The capacity to ask good questions also remains. A new platform can change the way information is
stored, models are deployed or agents are built, but I still need to understand what result is
expected, which constraints exist, what can fail, who needs to intervene and how it will be
determined whether the solution produced value.

Industrial Engineering allows me to recognize processes, flows, capacities, constraints and systemic
effects regardless of the platform. Industrial Design forces me to transform that complexity into
understandable and usable experiences. Data science provides the method for learning from evidence.
Architecture connects those elements within solutions capable of evolving.

My professional advantage is not in memorizing more tools than other people. It is in developing

When a new technology appears, I do not start from scratch. I arrive with a way of understanding
systems, a discipline for building evidence, a process for validating results and a knowledge
architecture that preserves what was learned before. That reduces the time needed to go from initial
familiarity to an applicable capability.

This chapter does not intend to demonstrate only that I learn fast. It demonstrates that I know how
to decide what to learn, turn it into a solution, test its limits, document how it works and
transform it into a capability that remains after the tool changes.
