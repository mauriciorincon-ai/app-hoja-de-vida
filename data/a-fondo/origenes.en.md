## Why I chose to study Industrial Engineering, and at which university

<!-- seccion: por-que-industrial -->

I chose to study Industrial Engineering because I wanted to understand how organizations work as
complete systems and, above all, how they could work better. I was interested in a discipline
that did not limit itself to observing an isolated part of reality, but that made it possible to
connect processes, people, information, technology, resources and decisions. In this branch of
engineering I found a rigorous way of turning complex problems into understandable structures
and of transforming that understanding into concrete, measurable and sustainable improvements.

Industrial Engineering taught me that an organization's results do not depend on a single element,
but on the relationships that exist between multiple components. A productivity problem can have
its origin in a poorly designed process, in information that arrives late, in ambiguous
responsibilities, in decisions that lack sufficient evidence, or in tools that do not respond to
the reality of the work. This systemic perspective became the foundation of my way of thinking
and continues to guide the way I analyze a company's challenges.

I studied it at the Pontificia Universidad Javeriana, in Bogotá, between 2009 and 2016. Ten years
after graduating I still start the same way I did then: before proposing a model, I draw the
process and locate where the flow is lost. Engineering gave me the habit of looking for the cause
in the relationship between the components, not in the component that fails, and no tool has
changed that habit.

## The emphasis in Data Analytics Intelligence: when I discovered data

<!-- seccion: el-enfasis-en-analitica -->

I took the degree with an emphasis in Data Analytics Intelligence, and that emphasis was a turning
point: it let me discover that data could extend engineering's traditional capacity to understand
and transform systems. It was no longer only a matter of describing a process or proposing an
improvement based on observation, but of modeling phenomena, identifying patterns, formulating
hypotheses, anticipating behaviors and supporting decisions with evidence.

That is where two dimensions that still define my professional profile began to converge. On one
hand, engineering gave me an integral view of processes, operations and organizations. On the
other, analytics allowed me to represent that reality through data, explore its relationships and
build solutions capable of generating actionable knowledge. I learned to use SQL, Python and Power
BI not as ends in themselves, but as instruments for making systems visible, understanding their
behavior and improving the quality of decisions. The program rested on process optimization, data
analysis and decision support, and on the modeling and prediction of industrial phenomena with
those three tools.

In the middle of the degree, between 2013 and 2014, I spent a year in Melbourne, Australia, in an
intensive English course that closed with the IELTS certification. That is where the B2 English
comes from, the one with which today I read technical documentation, write analytical reports and
hold a professional discussion.

Over time, that combination evolved from descriptive analysis and business intelligence toward
data engineering, the design of analytical models, the creation of intelligent applications and
the development of artificial intelligence solutions and agents. However, the principle remains
intact: before building a solution I need to understand the system in which it will have to
operate. Industrial Engineering gave me precisely that capacity to look beyond the technological
component and recognize the people, processes, constraints, incentives and decisions that
determine whether an initiative can generate real value.

## How industrial engineering defines my vision of artificial intelligence

<!-- seccion: la-ingenieria-y-la-ia -->

This training also deeply influences the way I conceive artificial intelligence. I do not look at
an AI agent as an independent feature or as a demonstration of what a technology can do. I
understand it as a new participant within a work system, with access to certain information,
delimited responsibilities, interactions with people and applications, specific controls and
consequences for the operation. That view lets me analyze not only how to build it, but where it
should intervene, which decisions it can support, which activities it can execute, which risks it
introduces and how it should be integrated into a reliable enterprise architecture.

Choosing Industrial Engineering was important because it gave me a vision that transcends any
tool and that remains valid even when the technology changes. It taught me to look for
relationships before isolated answers, causes before symptoms and sustainable capabilities before
temporary solutions. On that foundation I have built everything else: my depth in data and
analytics, my capacity to develop intelligent solutions and my vision for integrating artificial
intelligence into enterprise architectures oriented toward value, trust and transformation.

It is the same foundation with which today, since March 2025, I lead the artificial intelligence
strategy of Fundación CTIC. And it is the same one with which, between August 2023 and January
2025, I built at Vesting the platform that observed AI agents in production. In both cases the
starting question was not what the model can do, but what place it occupies within the work
system, what information it receives and who answers for what it does.

## The training in industrial design

<!-- seccion: la-formacion-en-diseno -->

In parallel, I took undergraduate studies in Industrial Design at the same university, between
2011 and 2016, with a focus on sustainability and on the cultural impact of design. This training
complemented my engineering thinking with a deeper understanding of the relationship between
people and solutions. While engineering taught me to optimize systems, design taught me to observe
the human experience within them, to question assumptions and to recognize that a technically
correct solution can fail if it turns out to be hard to understand, adopt or incorporate into
everyday life.

From Industrial Design I keep an idea that I apply constantly: a solution that people do not
understand or cannot use with confidence is not finished yet. Functionality is not enough. A
solution needs to communicate its purpose, respond to the context of whoever uses it and reduce
the complexity required to perform a task or make a decision. This conviction influences the way
I design information models, analytical experiences, applications and artificial intelligence
agents.

The program worked on the design of products and systems with a technological and market focus.
What stayed with me from those five years is the method: observe whoever is going to use
something before drawing it. It is the same habit I apply today to a Power BI dashboard or to the
interface of an agent. If the person who decides does not understand what they see, the problem
is the design's, not the person's.

## The convergence: engineering, data, design and artificial intelligence

<!-- seccion: la-convergencia -->

The convergence between Industrial Engineering, data analytics and Industrial Design ended up
building a professional perspective that I now consider essential. Engineering lets me understand
and structure complexity. Data lets me observe it and explain it with evidence. Design forces me
to turn that knowledge into understandable and usable solutions. Artificial intelligence finally
extends the capacity of those solutions to interpret information, assist people, coordinate
actions and transform the way work is executed.

That is why my career does not represent a departure from Industrial Engineering, but a natural
evolution of its principles. I went from studying how to improve processes to building analytical
capabilities that make it possible to understand them; from analyzing decisions to developing
solutions that can assist them; and from optimizing existing systems to designing data and
artificial intelligence architectures capable of transforming the way an organization operates,
learns and evolves.

The three kinds of training have dates: engineering and its emphasis in analytics, between 2009
and 2016; design, between 2011 and 2016; and applied artificial intelligence, since August 2023,
when at Vesting agents stopped being a subject of study and became the system that had to be
observed in production. What has no date is the method, because it has been the same since the
first day: understand the system, measure it and give someone the instrument to decide about it.

## The first job was about processes, not data

<!-- seccion: el-primer-trabajo -->

My first professional experience began in August 2016, at Inglopres, as a Process Engineer, and I
was there until June 2017. Inglopres rented, bought and sold heavy machinery, with a fleet of
about 120 units between machines and vehicles. At first sight, it might seem a starting point far
removed from data engineering, business analytics or artificial intelligence. In reality, it was
the setting in which I understood one of the ideas that has most defined my career: behind every
analytical or intelligent capability there is a process that must be understood, information that
must be structured and a decision that must be improved.

I came in with the responsibility of understanding how the organization worked and contributing
to integrating its processes through the implementation of an enterprise resource planning
system, an ERP: Odoo, in a new implementation. The challenge did not consist only of deploying a
platform. It was necessary to identify how the work was executed, harmonize definitions across
different areas, establish responsibilities, model information flows and translate the real
operation into structures that the system could represent consistently. I modeled the flows in
BPMN with Bizagi, and with FlexSim I simulated the operation to compare alternatives before
changing it.

This experience allowed me to understand that implementing an enterprise solution involves much
more than configuring technology. A system only acquires value when it adequately reflects the
organization's reality, connects processes that previously operated in a fragmented way and
generates information that can be used to coordinate activities, evaluate results and make
decisions. That is where my capacity to move between the language of the business and the
language of systems began to consolidate, a skill that would later prove fundamental in the
design of analytical solutions and artificial intelligence architectures.

The detail of that year —the ERP, the work study, the supply chain, the team of twelve people and
the 95% customer satisfaction— is in the Inglopres document. What matters here is the turn it
produced.

## The turning point: to improve the process I needed its data

<!-- seccion: el-punto-de-inflexion -->

The turning point appeared when I tried to analyze and improve the processes based on evidence. I
needed to observe their behavior, monitor operational metrics, identify deviations and establish
relationships between the activities and their results. However, I discovered that an important
part of the necessary data did not exist, was not captured with sufficient quality or was not
organized to answer the business questions. To improve the process I needed the process's data
and, to have that data, I first had to design the way to produce it.

I then had to define information structures, organize operational records and design databases
that would make it possible to turn everyday execution into analyzable evidence. The ERP had its
own database; the one that was missing was the one for analysis, and that one I built in SQLite,
with the entities, their relationships and the rules that preserved the meaning of each field, and
I queried it in SQL to produce the indicators. This lesson was decisive: data does not appear
spontaneously nor does it acquire value by the simple fact of being stored. It must be designed
from the origin, with a clear understanding of the phenomenon it represents, of the rules that
give it meaning and of the decisions it will have to support.

That is where I understood that the quality of an analysis begins long before a visualization. It
begins in the way an activity is defined, an event is recorded, an entity is identified, different
sources are related and the context needed to interpret a result is preserved. A reliable metric
is not born in a dashboard. It is born in a correctly modeled process, in adequately captured data
and in an architecture capable of preserving its meaning from beginning to end.

It was not a career decision. It was what the problem demanded: my career turned toward data
because a process cannot be improved without measuring it, and it cannot be measured without
first building its information. From there also came a combination of experimentation and
discipline that I have kept since 2016: freedom to discover better alternatives, and mechanisms
to evaluate, control and reproduce whatever proves its value.

## Measuring time is not understanding work: the time study and fatigue

<!-- seccion: medir-el-tiempo -->

The analysis of work also led me to understand that a process is not made up only of activities,
times and technical resources. It is executed by people whose capacities, rhythms and levels of
effort can vary according to the nature of the task and the conditions under which it is
performed. At Inglopres I did a formal time study of the repetitive activities —timing, rating of
the pace and calculation of the standard time— and I incorporated the analysis of the allowances
associated with fatigue, with the allowances from the ILO table, as a necessary element for
establishing realistic work times, better understanding the variability of performance and
preventing the pursuit of efficiency from turning into operational demands that were hard to
sustain.

This approach allowed me to recognize a fundamental difference between measuring time and
understanding work. An observed time does not automatically represent a fair, stable or
reproducible standard. To turn it into a useful reference, it is necessary to consider the
characteristics of the activity, the effort required, the conditions of the environment, the
repetitiveness, the recovery periods and the variability inherent in human execution. Efficiency
cannot be evaluated while ignoring the factors that make it possible to sustain it.

The fatigue allowances did not represent an arbitrary margin added to a measurement, but a way of
modeling a dimension of the process that could remain hidden if the analysis was limited to
average times and production volumes. Incorporating them made it possible to build more realistic
standards, to differentiate between operational deviations and variations explainable by the
conditions of the work, and to seek a more responsible balance between productivity, quality and
sustainability of performance.

This lesson broadened early on my way of interpreting data. I understood that a figure should never
be analyzed in isolation from the context that produces it. Two activities with similar execution
times can involve completely different levels of effort, complexity or risk. In the same way, an
apparent improvement in productivity can turn out to be unsustainable if it is obtained by
shifting pressure onto the team, reducing its capacity to recover or increasing the probability of
errors. Measuring correctly requires representing not only what happens, but also the conditions
under which it happens. Years later I turned that same problem into one of the seven
investigations I publish: the one on fatigue allowances and line balancing.

## From process to architecture: the early lesson of the first job

<!-- seccion: la-leccion-de-arquitectura -->

This first experience laid the foundation for my later evolution toward data engineering and
analytics. Over time, that need to monitor operational metrics turned into the capacity to design
data models, build semantic models, develop business intelligence solutions and create analytical
experiences oriented toward decision-making. However, the purpose remained the same: to make the
operational reality observable, understandable and transformable through reliable information.

The connection with my current work in artificial intelligence also arises from that same lesson.
An intelligent application or an AI agent cannot generate sustainable value if it is built on
ambiguous processes, data without context or business rules that only exist in the informal
knowledge of a few people. Before delegating a task to an intelligent system, it is necessary to
understand how it is executed, what information it requires, which decisions it involves, which
exceptions can arise and which controls must remain under human responsibility.

That is why that first enterprise implementation was also an early lesson in architecture. It
taught me that processes, data, applications and people cannot be designed as independent
components. Every decision about the operation determines what information is produced. Every
decision about the data conditions what can be analyzed. Every decision about the architecture
defines how reliable, scalable and governable a solution can become. That integrated understanding
became the common thread between my first projects of 2016 and the capabilities I develop today.

## ISO 9001:2015, the first school of quality and traceability

<!-- seccion: iso-9001-la-primera-escuela -->

My work at Inglopres also covered the supply chain and quality assurance under the ISO 9001:2015
standard. This environment strengthened a discipline that has remained throughout my career: it
is not enough to do something correctly; it is necessary to define what doing it well means,
demonstrate that it was met, keep evidence and establish a mechanism to repeat it consistently.

Quality management taught me to think in terms of criteria, controls, traceability,
responsibilities and continuous improvement. Years later, those same principles would become
essential elements of my work with data and artificial intelligence. A piece of data must be
traceable back to its origin. A metric must have a verifiable definition. A transformation must
be reproducible. A solution must be observable. An agent must operate within explicit limits. And
a decision assisted by artificial intelligence must preserve the evidence needed to understand
how it was produced.

ISO 9001 and ISO/IEC 42001 share the same high-level structure of ISO management systems. Applied
in 2016 to the quality of a heavy-machinery operation and in 2025 to the management of artificial
intelligence in a health institution, the structure is the same, and what is done leaves a
written trace in both. That continuity is the reason it feels natural to me today to lead the
implementation of a standard: I learned to work inside one from my first job.

## What the first job already contained

Seen in retrospect, my first professional experience already contained the central elements of my

That is why I do not consider that I moved from processes to data and, later, from data to
artificial intelligence. My evolution has consisted of progressively expanding my capacity to
connect those dimensions. I began by understanding how work functions, learned to represent its
reality through data, advanced toward analytical solutions that turn that data into decisions and
arrived at the design of applications and agents capable of using information, knowledge and
tools to assist with or execute part of that work.

My first job was formally a process role, but that is where my career in data, architecture and
intelligent solutions began. It was the place where I understood that every high-value
technological transformation requires a rigorous connection between the operational reality, the
people who make it possible, the information that represents it and the capability one wants to
build. That understanding continues to be the foundation on which I design analytical solutions,
intelligent applications, AI agents and enterprise architectures capable of transforming work
without losing sight of its human dimension. Eleven months at Inglopres were enough to pose the
problem; the nine years since have been different scales of the same answer.

## From process to indicator, to platform, to AI: the map in three paragraphs

<!-- seccion: del-proceso-a-la-ia -->

**From the process to the indicator** (Ceinfes and C&M Consorcio 2018). I stopped improving one
activity and moved to directing and supervising entire operations through their data: KPIs per
area, performance dashboards, a history so that each analysis did not start from zero.

**From the indicator to the platform** (Cafam, C&M Consultores and Banco Pichincha). Data stopped
being a report and became a system: the implementation of a WMS, an ETL pipeline and dashboards
that crossed heterogeneous sources, and in banking the hardest lesson of BI: a dashboard nobody
uses is not a deliverable, and adoption is measured.

**From the platform to AI** (Vesting and Fundación CTIC). At Vesting I built from scratch, in
Microsoft Fabric, the platform with which AI agents were monitored in production, and I documented
the replicable process for designing them. At CTIC I lead the institutional AI strategy under

Three leaps across eight organizations, between August 2016 and today; the sectors, counted one by
one, are at the end, in the count of the years.

## From the process to the indicator: Ceinfes (2017–2018)

<!-- seccion: del-proceso-al-indicador -->

Between November 2017 and November 2018, at Ceinfes, my work evolved from the direct improvement
of processes toward the construction of the information systems needed to understand, measure and
direct them. As Operations Coordinator I developed key indicators for logistics operations, data
digitization processes and human resources scheduling —three fronts serving more than 100 schools
a year— and contributed to the transition toward a process-based management model supported by
information. This change broadened my perspective: it was no longer only a matter of intervening
in an operation, but of building the analytical capacity with which the organization could observe
its performance, explain its results and decide how to transform it.

In that stage I understood that an indicator is not merely a figure presented in a report or a
visualization. It is an instrument designed to observe the behavior of a system. For it to be
truly useful it must represent a relevant phenomenon, have a precise definition, preserve its
traceability back to the source data and be associated with a possible decision or action. An
indicator that does not make it possible to interpret what is happening, identify a deviation or
decide how to respond ends up being decorative information, no matter how attractive its
presentation.

I then began to understand indicators as gauges distributed along a pipeline. Each one had to
observe a specific part of the flow and, at the same time, contribute to explaining the
performance of the whole system. It was not enough to measure the final result. It was necessary
to instrument the different stages to know what came in, what was transformed, how long it stayed
at each point, what volume moved forward, where work accumulated, what errors appeared, what
capacity was available and what conditions affected the quality of the output.

This view made it possible to differentiate result indicators from indicators capable of
anticipating and explaining the result. While the former showed what had already happened, the
latter made it possible to recognize early signals, identify constraints and act before a
deviation consolidated. That is where a discipline I keep to this day began: I do not design
isolated metrics, but measurement systems that establish relationships between demand, capacity,
flow, time, quality, productivity, service level and business result.

## Shared definitions and digitization as a production line

<!-- seccion: definiciones-y-digitalizacion -->

Creating indicators at Ceinfes required a deep understanding of the process to be measured. Before
formulating a metric, I needed to identify its units of analysis, relevant events, states,
operating rules, control points and exception conditions. I also had to agree on what each concept
meant for the different areas, because a figure can be technically correct and still lead to wrong
decisions if people interpret differently what it represents. This need to build shared
definitions was one of my first approaches to what I now understand as semantic modeling and
information governance.

The data digitization processes made this relationship between operation and information
especially visible. Although their result was a digital asset, their execution behaved like a
production line made up of interdependent stages. The reception and preparation of the inputs,
the capture, the validation, the quality control, the correction of inconsistencies and the final
consolidation formed a flow in which each point had a different capacity, processing time, level
of variability and impact on the result. In figures: some 250 answer sheets per session, two
scanning stations and, around them, the stations for preparation, organization, validation and
reordering; close to 10% of the sheets needed manual capture.

This context led me to incorporate line balancing as a way of analyzing and improving the
digitization process. The goal was not to maximize the productivity of each activity in isolation,
but to harmonize the capacities of the stages to favor the complete flow. A station could show a
high level of utilization and, at the same time, harm the overall performance if it produced more
work than the next one could absorb. Conversely, an apparently less productive activity could be
protecting quality or preventing errors from advancing toward stages where correcting them would
be more costly.

## Line balancing and indicators as sensors of the flow

<!-- seccion: indicadores-como-sensores -->

Balancing required observing how workloads were distributed, where accumulations formed, which
resources remained underutilized, which activities constrained the flow and how variability and
rework affected the effective capacity. This made it possible to move beyond a view based
exclusively on averages. Two stages with similar mean times could behave very differently
depending on the dispersion of their times, the complexity of the cases received, the frequency
of exceptions and the quality of the inputs. In the vocabulary of the theory of constraints, that
station is the bottleneck: the real pace of the process was its pace, not that of the fastest
one.

Indicators thus became sensors of the operational flow. The input volume made it possible to
understand demand. The inventory of pending work made accumulations visible. The cycle time showed
how long a case took to cross the system. The processing rate made it possible to observe the
effective capacity. The percentage of errors and rework revealed hidden losses of capacity.
Utilization helped identify pressures on resources. The distribution of loads made it possible to
evaluate whether the line was truly balanced. Each indicator answered a specific question, but
its greatest value emerged when they were interpreted as parts of the same system.

This approach also taught me that optimizing one stage in isolation can worsen the overall
result. Increasing the capture speed, for example, does not necessarily improve performance if it
increases errors, saturates validation or generates more rework. Real improvement appears when
quality, capacity and speed advance in a coordinated way. Since 2018 I have tried to avoid metrics
that incentivize local results at the expense of the integral functioning of the process.

## The measurement architecture: from the operational event to the dashboard

<!-- seccion: arquitectura-de-medicion -->

Starting from the experience at Ceinfes I began to conceive a measurement architecture in which
data travels through a pipeline as important as the operational flow itself. Events must be
captured correctly, validated, transformed, related to consistent definitions and organized into
structures that make it possible to analyze them. Only after that journey can they become
reliable indicators and analytical experiences capable of supporting decisions. The dashboard
represents the visible layer, but its credibility depends on everything that happens before a
figure reaches the screen.

This understanding represents one of the foundations of my current work with data engineering,
semantic models and Power BI. An enterprise analytical solution does not begin with the selection
of visualizations. It begins in the architecture that connects the sources with the
transformation processes, the quality controls, the business rules, the information models and
the metrics used by the organization. My goal is for each indicator to preserve its meaning along
that journey and to be traceable from the executive decision down to the operational event that
originated it.

At Ceinfes that chain ended every Friday at the board of directors, with a weekly report that came
out of the same KPIs of the operation and from which concrete decisions came: incentive programs,
improvements in working conditions, redesign of workstations, redesign of processes and redesign
of the software. It was the first time I saw an indicator travel the complete path, from the
operational event to the executive decision, and return to the operation turned into a change.

## From the indicator to decision systems: the supervision of TransMilenio (2018–2020)

<!-- seccion: del-indicador-a-la-plataforma -->

Between November 2018 and May 2020 I worked as a Junior Operations Analyst at C&M Consorcio 2018,
within the supervision of TransMilenio. This experience changed the scale of the systems I had to
understand. I went from analyzing delimited organizational processes to working with an urban
operation in which vehicles, drivers, routes, scheduling, demand, contractual compliance and
service quality interacted: some 150 routes operated by 10 concessionaire companies.

In this context, the data did not come from a single source nor did it describe reality from a
common perspective. Each system recorded a part of the operation —fare collection, the fleet and
the buses' GPS, service scheduling, incidents and the users' PQR (complaints and claims)—, with
different structures, rules, frequencies and levels of quality. To build a reliable view it was
necessary to integrate those sources, relate events and interpret each record within the process
that produced it. Data extraction, transformation and loading then stopped being an isolated
technique for me and became the mechanism for analytically reconstructing a complex system.

This experience taught me that integrating information does not mean only consolidating files or
connecting databases. It means producing a coherent representation of reality, capable of
preserving the relationships between the actors, resources and decisions that determine the
result. An operational assignment, for example, could not be understood by observing the driver,
the vehicle or the route separately. Its performance emerged from the interaction between these
dimensions, from the characteristics of demand and from the constraints inherent in the
operation.

That is where a view that would later be fundamental in my work with analytical models and
enterprise architectures began to consolidate: entities do not generate value separately. Value
appears when the model correctly represents their relationships. This understanding led me to
move beyond analyses based exclusively on independent lists and to think in terms of structures
capable of jointly representing the availability of resources, the characteristics of the
routes, the typology of the vehicles, the expected demand and the conditions under which a
decision had to be made.

## Convoys, assignment and the right instrument for each decision

<!-- seccion: el-instrumento-correcto -->

In the supervision of TransMilenio I also learned that some business problems cannot be solved
through descriptive analytics alone. Knowing what happened makes it possible to evaluate the
operation, but does not necessarily determine which combination of resources should be selected
to improve the result. Some decisions require prescriptive models capable of comparing
configurations, incorporating constraints and proposing alternatives aimed at maximizing
compliance, the capacity used or the value generated by the system.

The transport operation also allowed me to recognize phenomena that could remain hidden inside
aggregate indicators. An overall result could look acceptable while certain behaviors silently

Detecting these events was important, but it also revealed a limitation of traditional analytics:

This experience established a distinction that continues to guide my work: not every decision

models, recommendations or operational mechanisms capable of intervening at the right moment.
Choosing the right instrument requires understanding the frequency of the decision, how long the

be responsibly delegated. The two questions I left open in 2020 —the driver–bus–route assignment

## Risks, health data and lifecycle traceability

<!-- seccion: riesgos-y-datos-de-salud -->

In this context, artificial intelligence risk management does not represent a barrier to
innovation. It is the mechanism that makes it possible to move forward with greater clarity and

makes it possible to distinguish between controlled experimentation and unnecessary exposure.

Health-related information demands especially careful consideration. Behind every piece of data

responsibility.

I also consider it essential to differentiate the evidence obtained from institutional sources
from the results generated or inferred through artificial intelligence. A reliable solution must
make it possible to recognize which information was retrieved, which content was calculated,
which part was generated and which assumptions intervened in the answer. This distinction
protects the interpretation of the results and facilitates informed human intervention. It is the

## The thread: the method that unites the eight jobs

<!-- seccion: el-hilo -->

The thread that unites the eight jobs fits in one sentence: understand a process, measure it, and
give someone the instrument to decide about it. The scale changed and the tool changed; the
method is the same, and it has a name.

- **Time study and fatigue allowances**, at Inglopres, with the ILO table: an observed time is not
  a standard until it recognizes the effort of whoever performs it.
- **Line balancing and theory of constraints**, at Ceinfes: the bottleneck sets the real pace,
  and the effective capacity —with exceptions and rework— is not the theoretical one.
- **Process modeling in BPMN and simulation**: Bizagi at Inglopres, Ceinfes, Cafam and Banco
  Pichincha; FlexSim at Inglopres and Cafam. Draw the process as executed before touching it,
  and test the alternative before implementing it.
- **An assignment problem with constraints**, at TransMilenio: driver, bus and route are not
  three decisions but one, and the prescriptive starts by formulating it that way.
- **Kanban and Scrum**, since Ceinfes: visible work, with a limit on work in progress, and what
  is started gets finished before opening the next thing.
- **ISO 9001 and, today, ISO/IEC 42001**: the same high-level structure of ISO management
  systems, applied in 2016 to the quality of an operation and in 2025 to the management of
  artificial intelligence. What is done leaves a written trace, and that did not change.

That is why my career is not a departure from Industrial Engineering but its evolution: I went
from studying how to improve processes to building the analytical capabilities that make it
possible to understand them, and from analyzing decisions to building solutions that assist
them. An AI agent, seen this way, is not a feature: it is a new participant within a work system,
with access to certain information, limits and a responsibility that has to be designed.
