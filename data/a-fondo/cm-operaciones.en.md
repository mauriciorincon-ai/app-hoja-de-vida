## The operation of a city: the supervision of TransMilenio

<!-- seccion: la-operacion-de-una-ciudad -->

I joined C&M Consorcio 2018 in November 2018 as a Junior Operations Analyst, within the supervision of TransMilenio, and stayed there until May 2020: 18 months. This experience represented my entry into mass transit and the first scenario of my career in which I had to analyze an operation of urban scale, made up of multiple actors, services, vehicles, routes, contractual obligations and events produced continuously. In figures, the supervision covered some 150 routes grouped into 10 concessionaire companies.

Until then I had used data to understand and run delimited organizational processes: a process at Inglopres, an operation at Ceinfes. In the transit system, the challenge was different: I needed to rebuild the behavior of an extensive operation from records generated by diverse sources, with different levels of detail, timeliness and quality. No source contained on its own a complete representation of what was happening. Reality had to be built by relating scheduling, execution, routes, times, incidents and operating conditions. The sources, by name, were five:

- fare collection;
- the fleet and the buses' GPS;
- the scheduling of services;
- the operation's incidents;
- the users' PQR (complaints and claims).

Each one recorded a part of the operation with its own structure, its own frequency and its own quality, and each one arrived separately. Cross-referencing them was the job.

## Supervising with data: traceability as a condition of legitimacy

<!-- seccion: supervisar-con-datos -->

The word that best defines this experience is supervision. Our role did not consist in operating the system directly, but in evaluating with evidence whether the operation executed corresponded to the defined conditions: we verified with data that TransMilenio was being operated as agreed. This profoundly changed the nature of the data. The information was not used only to improve an internal process, but to sustain conversations between different actors, verify the fulfillment of commitments and establish a defensible interpretation of the results. The data stopped serving to improve your own process and went on to sustain a conversation between the authority and the concessionaire, where neither of the two parties could argue about where the number had come from.

In that context I understood that traceability is not only a technical characteristic. It is a condition of legitimacy. When an indicator can have operational, contractual or economic effects, it must be possible to explain what it represents, which sources it comes from, which rules were applied and which concrete events sustain its result. Trust could not depend on who presented the figure, but on the ability to rebuild it.

With 10 concessionaire companies on the other side of the table, that rule was not a methodological preference: every report could be disputed by ten actors with different interests, and the only argument that held up was the complete lineage of the data, from the GPS or fare-collection record to the figure in the report.

## The performance dashboards and reports

<!-- seccion: tableros-e-informes -->

I developed performance dashboards and reports aimed at strengthening the control and transparency of the operation: two weekly reports, a monthly consolidated one and the ones requested on demand. To do so, I had to transform large volumes of records into understandable indicators, identify relevant deviations and present a view that allowed moving from the individual data point to the general behavior of the system. What was verified in them:

- services scheduled versus performed;
- routes and coverage;
- frequencies and intervals between buses;
- execution times and compliance of the operation.

This experience taught me that a supervision dashboard cannot be limited to showing aggregate results. It must allow traversing each indicator from the executive view down to the operational event that sustains it. The synthesis eases the decision, but the detail preserves the possibility of validating, explaining and disputing a conclusion on the basis of evidence.

Transparency then acquired a concrete meaning: that the different actors could observe the same result, understand its definition and recognize how it had been calculated. When two parties argue permanently about the origin of a figure, the problem is not only in the visualization. It may be in the sources, the rules, the transformations or the absence of a shared definition. With 150 routes in the weekly report, an ambiguous definition of "service performed" turned into 150 arguments.

## The indicator as contractual evidence; outcome versus behavior

<!-- seccion: indicador-como-evidencia -->

In a supervised operation, the indicator was not only a management tool. It could also become evidence to evaluate the fulfillment of commitments and sustain contractual consequences: a deduction or a penalty could hang from a figure. This demanded an additional level of rigor: obtaining a reasonable result was not enough; it was necessary to preserve the relationship between the schedule, the observed execution, the rules applied and the conclusion presented. There I learned that, when a figure can affect responsibilities or payments, its lineage —the data lineage— must be as solid as its calculation.

There I consolidated a way of working that I later carried over to semantic models and enterprise solutions in Power BI. A metric must have a stable meaning, preserve its traceability and answer a relevant question. The dashboard is the interaction layer, but its reliability depends on the information architecture that connects the operational events with the analytical model and the decision.

I also learned to differentiate between outcome indicators and behavior indicators. The first —the lagging ones— allowed evaluating what had happened during a period. The second —the leading ones— helped understand how that result was taking shape, where irregularities appeared and which conditions could affect future performance. At TransMilenio, compliance in the monthly consolidated report was an outcome indicator; the interval between buses on a route, week by week, was a behavior indicator, and it was the one that warned earlier. This distinction was fundamental for later evolving from description toward prediction, recommendation and intervention.

## Automating reports and repetitive tasks: Excel, VBA and SQL

<!-- seccion: la-automatizacion -->

I implemented solutions to automate recurring activities of information preparation, validation and consolidation, reducing manual intervention and improving the consistency of processing. The tools were Excel and VBA for the scripts, and SQL on SQLite databases for the analyses: every week the concessionaires handed us their databases, and the records had to be extracted, validated, transformed with the same rules and consolidated into the cumulative dataset. That automation reduced processing time by at least 40%. Besides freeing up analytical time, it made it possible to apply the same rules repeatably and to reduce the dependence on individual procedures that are hard to audit.

However, automating did not consist only in executing an existing sequence faster. First it was necessary to identify which inputs the process received, which validations had to be performed, which rules transformed the data, which exceptions could arise and which result had to be produced. An automation built on ambiguous definitions processes inconsistencies faster, but does not correct them.

The order mattered: first the written rule, then the macro. With 10 concessionaires delivering databases with different structures, input validation —mandatory fields, possible ranges, duplicates— was the part of the script that changed most often and the one that prevented the most errors. A report that comes out fast but with a badly loaded database is worse than a late one, because the wrong figure is already on the table by the time it is discovered.

## The statistical memory: the operation's history

<!-- seccion: la-memoria-estadistica -->

The statistical databases became the analytical memory of the operation. They allowed comparing periods, identifying patterns, observing recurrences and distinguishing between an isolated event and a persistent behavior. Preserving that history was indispensable to understand the evolution of the system and to prevent each analysis from starting again from scratch. The concessionaires' weekly databases, organized in SQLite, became a cumulative dataset on which cumulative analyses could be done: the same route, the same interval, month after month.

Data stopped being used only to answer an immediate need and began to be preserved in a structured way to compare periods, recognize recurrences, identify patterns and distinguish between isolated events and persistent behaviors. I learned that an organization cannot improve cumulatively if each analysis starts from scratch or if the knowledge disappears when the person who produces it changes.

This stage strengthened my understanding of the data pipeline as a complete capability. The information had to be extracted, validated, transformed, related, stored and made available under consistent rules. It was my first complete data pipeline, years before doing it on a platform: later I would deepen this practice through modern data platforms, semantic models and analytical architectures, but here, between 2018 and 2020, I learned its fundamental principle: the result is only reliable when the journey that produces it is reliable too.

## Auditing compliance with the operating protocols

<!-- seccion: adherencia-a-protocolos -->

Part of my responsibility consisted in verifying adherence to TransMilenio's protocols and operating conditions. This involved contrasting execution with what had been scheduled or defined, using data to evaluate aspects such as services performed, routes, times and compliance of the operation. It is, in practice, a contractual compliance audit, and the indicators had economic consequences.

This function taught me to distinguish between formal compliance and systemic performance. An operation can individually satisfy several criteria and still produce a deficient result because of the interaction between its components. That is why, in addition to verifying whether a condition had been met, I needed to understand how the decisions about vehicles, drivers, routes and demand combined to produce the final result.

I also learned to distrust local optimizations. A decision could seem efficient for one vehicle, one route, one service or one time band and, at the same time, deteriorate the regularity, coverage or compliance of the complete system. I understood that analyzing an urban operation requires observing the interactions and the cumulative effects, not only the isolated performance of its components. The efficiency of one part does not guarantee the performance of the whole, and with 150 routes the sum of local efficiencies rarely matched the compliance of the system.

## The driver–vehicle–route assignment: a prescriptive question

<!-- seccion: asignacion-conductor-vehiculo-ruta -->

Resource assignment made this complexity especially visible. An available driver, an operational vehicle and a scheduled route do not constitute three independent decisions. They form an operational unit whose performance depends on the compatibility between their characteristics, the conditions of the route, demand and the constraints —and the failures— present during the service. Analyzing each element through separate lists could hide relationships decisive for compliance.

This understanding opened my interest toward analytics of a prescriptive nature. Historical information made it possible to know which combination had been used and what result it had produced. However, an operational decision also needs to evaluate which combination should be used to maximize compliance, the capacity mobilized or the use of resources under real constraints. The history said which combination had been used and what it had produced; deciding which one to use was another question, a prescriptive one, that the indicators did not answer.

The relationship between driver, vehicle, route, demand and operating conditions showed me that many decisions cannot be tackled through independent lists or indicators. Their result depends on the compatibility and the constraints that exist among all the elements of the system. I detected it in the TransMilenio data between 2018 and 2020; the formulation as a constrained assignment problem, and its answer, came later.

## Bus convoys (bus bunching) and the action policy

<!-- seccion: convoyes-de-buses -->

Another relevant phenomenon was the formation of clusters or convoys of vehicles: the bunching up of buses, bus bunching. By analyzing the temporal proximity between passes, it was possible to recognize situations in which several buses of the same route began to run with intervals that were too short. Even though each vehicle kept providing the service, the system lost interval regularity —the headway—: some users found several vehicles together and others faced longer waiting times.

Detecting the phenomenon was only the first level of the problem. The most important challenge consisted in turning detection into an action policy. If the organization can observe an anomaly but does not have an applicable instruction to respond while it is happening, the analysis remains separated from the operation. This experience led me to understand that the complete sequence must connect detection, explanation, recommendation, decision and intervention.

I also identified that some rules conceived to protect the operation could limit the capacity to correct it. A uniform instruction may be appropriate under normal conditions, but insufficient when the vehicles are already bunched and require differentiated actions depending on their position within the convoy: hold, overtake or skip stops depending on the place each bus occupies in the group. This reinforced my interest in context-conditioned policies, capable of responding to the real state of the system instead of always applying the same instruction. In 2019 this was a visible anomaly on an interval dashboard; from the supervision side, I had no way to turn it into a control rule.

## From the indicator to action: inform, recommend or execute

<!-- seccion: del-indicador-a-la-accion -->

These lessons anticipated a question that is central today in my work with applications and artificial intelligence agents: how far a solution should limit itself to informing, and when it can recommend or execute an action. An indicator makes a deviation visible. An alert directs attention. A model proposes a response. An application organizes execution. An agent can coordinate tools and act within defined limits. The appropriate level depends on the risk, the timeliness, the verifiability and the supervision required.

At this point I began to recognize different levels of analytical capability. An indicator allows observing. An alert directs attention toward a relevant condition. A predictive model anticipates a probable behavior. A prescriptive model compares alternatives and recommends a response. An application can organize execution and an intelligent solution can act within defined limits. Although those capabilities would be developed in greater depth in later stages of my career, the questions that made them necessary began to arise in this experience, in the supervision of TransMilenio.

The analysis of phenomena such as the formation of bus clusters allowed me to understand that detecting an anomaly is just the beginning. The real value appears when the information makes it possible to explain the phenomenon, evaluate alternatives and guide an applicable intervention while the possibility of changing the result still exists. A convoy that appears in the following week's report can no longer be dissolved; the same convoy detected while it is forming, with an applicable interval rule, can.

## The two questions I could not answer then, and that are research today

<!-- seccion: las-dos-preguntas -->

From the data of those 18 months came two questions that I detected in the operation and had no way of solving from the supervision side.

The driver–vehicle–route assignment. An available driver, an operational bus and a scheduled route are not three independent decisions: they form a unit whose performance depends on the compatibility between their characteristics, the route, demand and the failures that appear during the service. Modeling the failure as a property of the driver–bus pair, and not of each one separately, is what changes the assignment.

The bunching up of buses, bus bunching. By looking at the temporal proximity between passes, one could see when several buses of the same route started to run with intervals that were too short. Each bus kept providing service, but the system lost interval regularity: some users found several buses together and others waited twice as long. Detecting it was the first level; the hard part was turning detection into an interval control policy, because a uniform instruction is no use when the buses are already running together.

Those two questions are today two of the seven research pieces I publish in the showcase: the assignment of driver and bus with failures, formulated with integer and constraint programming on open data from the transit system, and the control of bus bunching, studied with discrete-event simulation and a validated convoy detector. What in 2019 was an anomaly on a dashboard is now a model with its method and its result. And there is a third line that was born from the same career path but from another job: the replacement order of an ERP, which comes from Cafam, not from here.

## What this role prepared: supervision, transparency and analytical memory

<!-- seccion: lo-que-preparo -->

Seen in retrospect, C&M Consorcio 2018 was my first immersion in operational data of urban scale. There I learned to integrate heterogeneous sources, build traceable indicators, automate information processes, preserve statistical memory and translate individual events into a coherent representation of a system's behavior. Scale stopped depending only on the volume of records and began to express itself in the number of relationships, actors, rules and consequences that had to be represented correctly to understand the operation: 150 routes, 10 concessionaires, five sources.

I also understood that supervision demands a different analytical discipline. The data must be solid enough to sustain conversations between actors with different responsibilities, interests and obligations. Every conclusion needs evidence, every transformation must be explainable and every indicator must preserve the link with the operating conditions it represents. When a figure can sustain the evaluation of commitments or generate contractual and economic consequences, its lineage must be as rigorous as its calculation.

This experience taught me that transparency does not consist only in allowing several parties to consult the same result. It requires that they can understand its definition, rebuild its provenance and recognize the rules through which the operational records were turned into a conclusion. That discipline anticipated several of the principles I would later apply in data governance, semantic models and analytical platforms: shared definitions, controlled transformations, verifiable evidence and a single defensible interpretation of the result.

Automation and the building of statistical databases also allowed the operation to develop an analytical memory, and each analysis to stop starting from scratch. That history, stored in SQLite and fed every week, was the asset that remained when I left in May 2020.

## What this role prepared: from description to prescription

<!-- seccion: de-la-descripcion-a-la-prescripcion -->

This stage progressively broadened the type of questions I could ask. First I needed to determine what had happened. Then, to understand why it had happened. Finally, I began to ask myself which resource assignments could produce better results and which policies would make it possible to respond in time to anomalous conditions. There my interest arose in advancing from descriptive analytics toward predictive and prescriptive capabilities, and there appears the transition I later traveled at C&M Consultores, with a demand forecasting model, and at Banco Pichincha, with predictive models in production.

C&M Consorcio 2018 taught me to read an urban operation through its data. I learned that observing a system is much more than producing indicators: it requires rebuilding its relationships, identifying the mechanisms that explain its behavior, preserving evidence about the transformations and recognizing which information is missing in order to intervene in it with greater precision.

This experience established several foundations of my current work. The integration of sources would evolve toward data pipelines and platforms. The need for consistent definitions would become semantic modeling and information governance. The automation of processing would open the way toward more intelligent applications. The questions about assignment and intervention would turn into predictive and prescriptive problems, and into two of the seven research pieces I publish. The demand for traceability would end up extending to the evaluation and observability of artificial intelligence solutions and agents.

This foundation prepared my later evolution toward more integrated, governed and action-oriented analytical solutions, without attributing to this stage capabilities I would develop later. At C&M Consorcio 2018 I learned to rebuild the system to make it observable. The following experiences would allow me to turn that observation into an architecture of data, decisions and progressively more advanced solutions.
