## What you are using right now

<!-- seccion: lo-que-estas-usando -->

If this answer was generated by the chat on this page, you are using a real implementation of
the architecture I describe. It is a retrieval-augmented generation system —RAG— built by me to
answer questions about my career from the versioned content published on CV Viva, and from that
content only.

It is not an isolated example or a demo prepared for a presentation. It is a capability
integrated into a public application since July 2026, used by real people and subject to real
constraints of availability, cost, latency, security, updating and user experience. Each answer
is assembled from the four most relevant sources that retrieval hands to the model, and each of
those sources navigates to a visible section of the site.

Operating this capability demands decisions that a prototype can avoid: what information is
authorized, how it is retrieved, when the model should be called, what happens if the provider
fails and how each answer can be verified. All of those decisions are written down —three of
them as architecture decision records in the repository: the provider adapter, the index with
its retrieval, and the entry of the showcase sheets into the index— and all of them can be
checked against the chat's behavior.

The chat thus turns my own CV into an observable artificial intelligence product. Each answer
lets you evaluate directly how I design retrieval, grounding, guardrails, citation, controlled
degradation and cost management. With a budget of US$20 per month and an observed real cost of
US$0, it also shows that a public generative capability can be sustained without the bill being
what decides the architecture.

## Why the model is not the system's memory

<!-- seccion: el-modelo-no-es-la-memoria -->

The model's job is not to remember my career or to improvise a persuasive version of it. Its
responsibility is to organize and explain the evidence retrieved from the site. The source of
knowledge is the published content; the model acts as an interpretation layer conditioned by
that content.

This separation protects the integrity of the system. My profile can evolve through controlled
changes to the files that represent it —the résumé data in YAML, the sheets of the 32 showcase
pieces and the documents of this knowledge base— without depending on retraining a model or
waiting for a provider to have absorbed recent information. When an experience, certification
or project changes, the update happens at the source and propagates to the index during the
next build of the site.

The architecture reflects a fundamental decision: generative intelligence must not take the
place of verifiable knowledge. It should help find it, relate it and communicate it without
breaking the link to its provenance. That is why the generative provider is interchangeable by
configuration among five adapted ones, and why none of them stores anything: telemetry records
provider, model, milliseconds and tokens, never the content of the conversation or the model's
output.

It is the same rule I apply on a data platform: a data point does not become reliable when it
shows up on the screen, but during the journey that prepares, validates and contextualizes it.
Here, the chat's answer does not begin when the model steps in. It begins in the quality of the
content, in the structure of the index and in the preservation of its metadata.

## The knowledge is built before the question

<!-- seccion: el-indice -->

The knowledge the chat uses is compiled during the build of the site, not at the moment a person
asks a question. A build script walks the authorized sources —the résumé data, the pipeline's
apps, the showcase sheets and the approved documents of this base—, extracts their contents and
produces an index separated by language, Spanish and English, with chunks, titles, metadata and
navigable destinations.

Each chunk keeps a reference to the section or page it comes from. Retrieval does not hand the
model text alone. It also supplies the identity of the source and the destination that will later
let the user verify the claim within the site.

The chunk size is decided and measured: at most 180 words. A longer subsection is split into
windows that keep the same anchor, because a long chunk enters the context whole and competes
with itself; with that cap, the worst case of one answer's context is bounded at about 720 words.
Before the rewrite of this base, the published index had 28 chunks per language; with the 25
approved documents and the showcase sheets it reaches 494. The number is printed by every build
and is a regenerated artifact, not something edited by hand.

This decision shifts toward the build process activities that do not need to be repeated on
every query. The content is analyzed, split and validated once per published version. The
question can then concentrate on retrieving the pertinent chunks and building the answer,
without reprocessing the whole corpus.

## The index is an inspectable artifact and a quality gate

<!-- seccion: indice-puerta-de-calidad -->

The index is an inspectable artifact. It can be opened, compared between versions and put under
test: it is one JSON file per language, generated on every build, that the server reads from
disk. This property matters because it turns retrieval into an observable part of the system,
not an opaque capability managed exclusively by an external provider.

The build also works as a quality gate. If a source has an incompatible structure, if a document
does not validate its schema, if a subsection identifier is repeated, if it lacks a valid
destination or produces a broken reference, the process detects the inconsistency before
publishing. The valid destinations are not listed by hand: they are derived from the sections of
the HOME and from the real routes of the site, so that a removed section leaves the documents
that pointed to it blind —and red. I would rather the build fail visibly than deploy a chat that
answers using chunks that are incomplete, badly identified or impossible to verify.

This design relates to the way I conceive analytical pipelines: each stage receives an input,
applies a transformation and leaves evidence of what it did. Published knowledge is therefore
treated as a versioned asset. Each change in the corpus can modify retrieval and must be
relatable to a version of the site. This traceability makes it easier to investigate why an
answer changed and to tell whether the difference came from the source, from the search
mechanism, from the instructions or from the generative provider.

A concrete example of that traceability: the 180-word window was not chosen by intuition. It was
measured on the real corpus, where the typical subsection had 143 words and the longest 247, and
at 180 only 4% of the subsections get split. Changing that number is a versioned change, with its
measurement beside it.

## The 32 showcase sheets enter the index, at weight 0.5

<!-- seccion: fichas-en-el-indice -->

Until September 2026 the index was assembled from the résumé data and from the documents of this
base. The 32 showcase pieces —six apps with brochure and complement, 13 agents, 7 research pieces
and 6 dashboards— have sheets with a promise, figures with provenance, limits, "nevers" and
blocks, and the chat could not see them: when someone asked about a specific piece, the answer
came from the prose I wrote about the showcase, not from the sheet.

Indexing them as-is produced the predictable result: 226 new chunks, all rich in vocabulary, and
the bank of 131 outside questions dropped from 102 to 94 hits in the top-4. The sheets were
winning the context from the documents on questions that were not about a piece: "does he have a
graduate degree?" brought up the sheet of the Graduate Assistant, and "what level does he have
with Power BI?" brought up the one for the Power BI Dashboard Builder.

The solution was a per-chunk weight that multiplies the search score. The owner's voice is the
documents; a sheet is evidence of one specific piece and should win only when the question is
about that piece. The number came from measuring the bank on the complete index of 494 chunks:

| Variant                   | Expected source in the top-4 | Questions with a sheet in the context |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------: | ------------------------------------: |
| without sheets            |                      102/131 |                                     0 |
| sheets at weight 1        |                       94/131 |                                    49 |
| sheets at weight 0.7      |                      100/131 |                                    26 |
| **sheets at weight 0.5**  |                  **102/131** |                                **11** |
| sheets at weight 0.35     |                      103/131 |                                     8 |

At 0.5 the bank recovers exactly the number it had without sheets, and eleven questions —the
showcase ones— still receive a sheet. Going lower buys nothing more and makes them invisible. The
sheets are not edited to index better: they belong to other houses, and adding one to the
showcase puts it into the chat on the next build, without writing new prose.

## Lexical retrieval is an architecture decision

<!-- seccion: recuperacion-lexica -->

Retrieval uses lexical search through MiniSearch —the BM25 algorithm— and does not currently
depend on embeddings or on a vector database. This choice is neither an accidental limitation
nor an incomplete stage of the system. It is a recorded architectural decision, proportional to
the size, structure and purpose of the corpus.

The content of CV Viva contains titles, section names, dates, companies, certifications,
projects and technical terms with a high capacity to discriminate. For this class of
information, a well-configured lexical search can retrieve pertinent results through matches,
field weighting and tolerance for typing errors.

The concrete configuration: the title weighs twice as much as the body, there is prefix matching
and a fuzzy tolerance of 0.2 for typing errors. That tolerance serves to assemble the model's
context and for the local search of the fallback, where what matters is not to lose a relevant
chunk. The scope guardrail, on the other hand, uses a second, strict search, with no fuzzy
tolerance: a word that differs by one letter from "data" never matches "data". They are two
modes over the same index, with different purposes: high recall to answer, high precision to
decide whether to answer.

The solution especially favors titles and other fields capable of expressing the purpose of each
chunk. Not every term should contribute the same weight. A match in the title of an experience
or certification can be more significant than several incidental appearances inside a long
paragraph.

The approach also offers determinism and reproducibility. Under the same version of the index and
the same configuration, a query produces the same ranking of results. This property makes it
easier to build control questions, investigate retrieval failures and clearly evaluate the effect
of each modification. And it introduces no variable costs for generating embeddings, needs no
synchronization with an external vector store and reduces operational dependencies: a query
costs zero.

## The stopwords were calibrated by measuring

<!-- seccion: stopwords-medidas -->

A lexical search is only as good as its list of stopwords. Without it, "en", "de" or "the" match
the whole corpus and any question looks pertinent. The first list was the obvious one; the second
batch came out of a measurement, not an intuition.

With the corpus of 28 chunks, a five-word off-topic question —a request for pet humor,
which shares no noun with the corpus— scored 4.28 and passed the guardrail. The only term that
matched was the preposition "sobre" ("about"), present in 42 of the 162 chunks of the index at
that time, and it sustained a false positive on its own. The automated out-of-scope test could not
see it because it asked the same request with the preposition "de", which was already on the list.

Into the list then went the prepositions that are not topic —"sobre", "desde", "entre", "hasta",
"sin", "tras"— and the imperatives aimed at the assistant —"escribe", "hazme", "dime" ("write",
"make me", "tell me")—. They are instructions, not topic, and their only effect was to add score
to off-topic questions. That correction was made with its measurement beside it, and the test
that previously could not see the case now sees it.

It is a small lesson that holds for any RAG: the vocabulary a question shares with the corpus is
not always content. Part of it is grammar, and grammar gets measured and discarded.

## Embeddings: a conditional evolution, not an automatic improvement

<!-- seccion: embeddings-condicionados -->

The rule applied is the same one I use in my applications pipeline —rule 13, code first—: do not
bring in artificial intelligence by default. Before increasing complexity, I must show which
feature of the problem demands a different solution and what verifiable improvement it would
produce.

Embeddings remain a possible evolution, not an automatic improvement. Adding them would make
sense if a representative set of questions showed that lexical search fails systematically at
retrieving paraphrases, semantic relationships or concepts expressed with sufficiently different
vocabularies. Today that set exists —136 questions written from outside— and it does not show
that failure: with four sources per answer, none is left without its own.

In that case, the most reasonable evolution would not necessarily be to replace the current
mechanism completely. It could consist of a hybrid retrieval that combined the precision of
explicit terms with the semantic capacity of vectors. The decision would have to be evaluated
through retrieval quality, latency, cost, maintainability and the ability to explain why each
chunk was selected.

The option is declared in public: "retrieval with embeddings" is one of the two votable features
on the CV Viva roadmap, alongside conversation memory. Anyone visiting the site can vote for it,
and the vote counts for real. What is not going to happen is that it gets in because it is
customary in RAG architectures.

I prefer an architecture advanced enough to solve the problem and understandable enough to be
governed. Sophistication only creates value when it improves a demonstrable result.

## Retrieving is not yet answering

<!-- seccion: recuperar-no-es-responder -->

A RAG architecture holds at least two different responsibilities: locating evidence and building
an answer based on it. Keeping them separate allows evaluating more precisely where an error
originates.

Retrieval must select pertinent chunks: here, the 4 most relevant, numbered. Generation must use
only those chunks to answer. A deficient result can occur because the index did not contain the
information, because the query did not retrieve the right chunk, or because the model ignored,
misinterpreted or exaggerated the available evidence.

This separation avoids indiscriminately attributing every failure to the model. If retrieval does
not supply the right source, even an advanced model can produce an incomplete answer. If the
evidence is sufficient and the answer introduces additional claims, the problem lies in the
control of generation.

That is why evaluation must observe both layers. For retrieval, what matters is whether the
relevant chunks appear among the first results and whether irrelevant content stays out; that is
what the golden set and the question bank measure on the real index, with the same search module
that runs in production. For the answer, what matters is whether the claims are supported,
whether the citations correspond to them and whether the system properly declares the gaps.

The final answer must also be evaluated as an experience. A technically correct retrieval can
produce a barely useful answer if the content is presented without hierarchy, repeats chunks or
does not clearly identify the conclusion. Quality requires solid foundations and an
understandable organization of the information.

This dimension connects with my training in Industrial Design. The user does not interact with
the index, the prompt or the provider. The user interacts with an answer. The architecture must
hide unnecessary complexity without hiding the provenance, the limitations or the mechanisms
needed to verify it.

## Out of scope before consuming tokens

<!-- seccion: fuera-de-alcance -->

The chat was designed to answer about my career, experience, projects, capabilities and content
published on CV Viva. It does not claim to work as a general assistant or to use the model's
reach to answer questions foreign to the site's purpose.

The first control layer operates before calling the generative provider. The query is compared
against the index in strict mode and, if no chunk reaches the relevance threshold, the system
answers with a fixed, bilingual message that explains the available scope: 0 tokens consumed, 0
calls to the provider.

This decision avoids spending on questions the system should not answer. It also reduces the
risk of the model using its general knowledge to improvise content that does not belong to the
authorized base.

The out-of-scope answer does not try to look smart. Its function is to honestly delimit what the
application can do and to steer the user toward questions compatible with its purpose. In a
governed architecture, acknowledging the limit is worth more than producing a plausible answer
on any topic.

This mechanism also protects the budget. A question foreign to the domain —"is it going to rain
in Madrid tomorrow?"— is resolved without using the provider, and a sequence of irrelevant
queries should not automatically turn into generative consumption.

The principle applies to larger-scale enterprise systems: the first decision of an intelligent
solution should not always be what to answer, but whether it is authorized and sufficiently
informed to answer.

## The threshold is measured, and the finding is negative

<!-- seccion: umbral-medido -->

The threshold must be evaluated carefully. If it is too low, irrelevant questions can reach the
model. If it is too high, valid queries can be rejected. That is why its setting was checked
against representative questions, linguistic variations, typing errors and indirect phrasings:
the whole bank, with its 15 off-topic questions, against the complete corpus.

The finding was negative, and I prefer to say it with its numbers. The worst-scoring legitimate
question gives 5.92 —"does he know DAX?"— and the best-scoring off-topic one gives 16.90 —a
request to write a function in another language that sorts a list—. Between those two numbers
live 30 legitimate questions. Any threshold that blocks the highest off-topic one takes a third of
the good ones down with it, and it is measured: with the threshold at 7, ten legitimate questions
receive "that one escapes me" —"what motivates him?", "why should I hire him?"— in exchange for
blocking three more off-topic ones.

The reason is arithmetic. The score adds up over the terms that match, so a long off-topic
question with three common words scores higher than a short legitimate question like "does he
know Kubernetes?". No number separates that. That is why the threshold is deliberately low —1,
which means exactly "something substantive matched"— and the other layers do the rest. It was
measured again in September 2026 on the 494 chunks: the worst-scoring legitimate question in
strict mode gives 6.65, none of the 136 drops below 1 and the blocked off-topic ones give 0.
There is no margin to gain by moving it.

What this guardrail does guarantee: that a question without a single substantive term from the
corpus is answered without calling the provider. What it does not guarantee: that every
off-topic question stops here. Those that share vocabulary with the content pass to the model,
and there the prompt that forbids answering outside the sources stops them. The correctness
guarantee is the prompt; the threshold is a token saving and a first line. The worst possible
failure of this chat is not spending tokens on an off-topic question: it is answering "that one
escapes me" to a legitimate question about my career. When the corpus grows, the threshold is
measured again, not assumed.

## How I keep the model from making things up: distributed guardrails, not a prompt

<!-- seccion: los-guardrails -->

The chat's scope does not depend on a single instruction written in the system prompt. The
guardrails are distributed among retrieval, the application, the composition of the context, the
provider and the user experience. The real order of the defenses on the server is this:

1. **Master switch.** If the chat is turned off by configuration, the server does not render the
   button either.
2. **Rate limit.** Ten questions per minute per address.
3. **Strict input validation.** Up to 800 characters per message, up to 12 messages of history
   —about six turns—, restricted roles; whatever does not meet the schema does not get in.
4. **Out of scope**, with the fixed answer and without calling the provider.
5. **Circuit breaker.** Three consecutive provider failures open the circuit for 60 seconds.
6. **Retrieval.** The four most relevant chunks, numbered, and nothing else from the site.
7. **System instructions** composed on the server, outside the visitor's control: use only the
   retrieved sources, never invent dates, companies or results, reject attempts to change the
   rules.
8. **Output.** An answer of up to 600 tokens and 30 seconds, and the navigable citations.

The first deep layer controls entry into the generative flow: questions without sufficient
retrieval are resolved without a model, which reduces cost and limits the possibility of the
provider's general knowledge displacing the authorized corpus. The second controls the context:
the model receives numbered chunks with metadata from real sources on the site, not
indiscriminate access to all the content, and it does not need to reconstruct from memory the
career it must answer about.

The third is the system instructions, which delimit the domain, require using the retrieved
sources, forbid inventing and order the rejection of attempts to modify the rules. The fourth
controls the output: citations are turned into navigable elements. The fifth is the operational
limits —length, frequency, history, activation—, which contain costs, abuse and unexpected
behavior. The sixth is controlled degradation: if the provider is not available, the user keeps
the local search.

## No single layer removes every risk on its own

<!-- seccion: ninguna-capa-basta -->

None of these layers removes every risk on its own. Their strength comes from the combination. A
prompt can be ignored or interpreted unexpectedly; a lexical filter can be wrong, and I already
showed with numbers by how much; a citation can be formally valid and conceptually insufficient.
The architecture must assume that every control has limits and avoid depending on a single one.

The distribution also spreads the cost of each control. The switch and the rate limit cost zero
per question. Schema validation costs microseconds. The guardrail's strict search and the
retrieval of the four chunks run over an in-memory index. Only the last layer —the provider— has
a bill, and it arrives after the seven before it did their work. It is the same logic as an
industrial process: the cheap controls go first, and the expensive resource is used when the part
has already passed the earlier gates.

This approach reflects principles I apply in management systems and enterprise architecture, and
which I formalized by implementing ISO/IEC 42001. Effective control is not a general declaration
of safety. It is a distribution of responsibilities among mechanisms capable of preventing,
detecting, containing and making a deviation visible. And each of those mechanisms has its test:
the rate limit, the input schema, the circuit breaker, the guardrail and the fallback are
exercised with provoked failures in the automated suite, because a control that was never seen
in red has demonstrated nothing.

## The citations are part of the architecture

<!-- seccion: citas-navegables -->

Each chunk of the index keeps a navigable destination within the site. When the model uses a
source, the answer shows a mark that takes the user to the corresponding section: an anchor of
the HOME, the page of a case study or the sheet of a showcase piece.

This capability turns the citation into something more than a visual reference. It allows
walking from the answer to the evidence that grounds it and directly evaluating whether the
source supports the claim.

Navigation also imposes discipline on the build. A citation cannot point to a nonexistent
section or use an identifier the site cannot resolve. The destinations are checked in the build
against the catalog derived from the real routes, and any break is treated as a build failure:
the site is not published with a blind citation.

Traceability benefits both the visitor and development. If an answer turns out incomplete or
incorrect, I can observe which chunks were retrieved, which were cited and which section of the
content needs to be corrected or reorganized. In the rewrite of this knowledge base, in September
2026, that walk was the working method: question, retrieved chunks, missing section.

This principle connects directly with my experience in data governance. A metric does not become
reliable by carrying a provenance label if the source does not contain the data or the rule that
supports it. In the same way, a generative answer does not become verifiable by including a
decorative citation.

## The exact truth about the source chips

<!-- seccion: la-verdad-de-los-chips -->

The presence of a citation does not guarantee quality on its own. The chunk must be pertinent and
truly support the claim. That is why the tests must verify not only that references appear, but
that a valid relationship exists between the answer and the evidence. And on that point I prefer
to write what there is, not what sounds better.

The chips that accompany an answer are the four sources that retrieval handed to the model, and
each one navigates to its section of the site. There is no output validator today that checks
that the model used each cited source in each sentence; there is the test that no citation can
point to a nonexistent destination, and there is the prompt that obliges the model to number the
sources it uses. The relationship between the claim and the evidence is verified today by whoever
reads, with the destination one click away.

I could have built the validator —it is a small sprint— or I could have written that it exists. I
decided to write the truth and leave the validator as declared future work. A site that demands
provenance for every figure on its dashboards cannot describe its own chat with a layer it does
not have.

The system's promise can then be expressed in a simple and exact way: you do not have to trust
the answer only because it was produced by a model. You can walk it back to the 4 sources the
model had in front of it and evaluate it yourself. For that, the destination has to exist, and
that is guaranteed on every build.

## The provider is interchangeable; the knowledge is not

<!-- seccion: proveedor-intercambiable -->

The architecture separates the generative provider from the knowledge and from the application's
main logic. The authorized content, the index, retrieval and the fundamental rules stay under the
system's control, while the provider fulfills a delimited function of synthesis and generation.

The configuration allows selecting, with one environment variable and without touching code,
among five providers previously adapted and integrated on the Vercel AI SDK: Groq —the initial
one, with Llama 3.3 70B, chosen in July 2026 for its speed and its free quota—, Gemini 2.5 Flash,
Azure through Microsoft Foundry (formerly Azure AI Foundry), Claude —with Haiku 4.5 by default—
and any service compatible with the OpenAI API, self-hosted ones included. The switch is made
through configuration and does not require modifying the chat's core logic. The provider changes;
the facts it answers about do not.

The main benefit is reducing coupling. The application should not cease to exist because a
provider changes, a quota is modified or a specific model disappears. The architecture needs to
preserve the ability to replace the dependency without rebuilding the knowledge or the entire
experience.

Nor does the provider become the application's official memory. It can change the way it
organizes an answer, but it must not modify the facts it answers about. That responsibility
belongs to the versioned content and to the retrieval mechanism.

## Interchangeable does not mean equivalent: the adaptation layer

<!-- seccion: capa-de-adaptacion -->

That the provider is chosen by configuration does not mean that any service can be incorporated
automatically or that all models produce equivalent results. Each provider has different formats,
limits, models, policies, context windows and behaviors. Interchangeability depends on an
adaptation layer —the only module in the repository that knows concrete providers— and on tests
that show the selected provider preserves the system's minimum conditions: grounding, numbered
citations, out-of-scope rejection, bounded answer.

That layer does more than translate formats: it decides what happens when a credential is
missing. If the configured provider lacks its key or its model, the application does not fail
silently or in production: the chat enters the same degradation mode it would use during an
outage, and since sprint 3 every credential also passes a smoke test before anything is built
against it.

This decision relates to enterprise artificial intelligence architecture. Models evolve quickly
and external dependencies can change. Designing a sustainable solution requires separating what
belongs to the organization, such as its data, definitions and rules, from what can be replaced,
such as the generative service used to process them. On Microsoft Foundry, on Groq or on a
self-hosted model, the index of 494 chunks and the eight server defenses are exactly the same.

## The system degrades without hiding it

<!-- seccion: degradacion-controlada -->

I do not use the expression "it never goes down" because no architecture can promise absolute
availability. What I did design was a controlled degradation that preserves a useful function
when the generative provider is unavailable or cannot complete the request.

Facing a failure, an exhausted quota, an expired timeout or a circuit opened after three
consecutive failures, the server answers with a 503 code that the interface recognizes, and
switches to a local search in the browser over the same index: it presents the most relevant
chunks, saying that it is a fallback and not an answer from the model. The user loses the
generative synthesis but keeps access to the knowledge. It is the same asset with two consumers:
the server reads it from disk on every question, and the browser downloads it only when it needs
it, so it does not weigh on the site's initial load.

The interface communicates this change explicitly. It does not present a local search as if it
had been produced by the model, nor does it hide the reduction in capability. Honesty about the
state of the solution is part of its experience and of its governance.

This behavior was designed because the chat's essential function is not to generate prose, but to
facilitate verifiable access to the site's information. If generation fails and the evidence
remains available, the application can still fulfill an important part of its purpose. The first
time it happened for real was not a drill: in sprint 3, a provider credential returned a 401 in
the integration, the circuit opened and the fallback worked exactly as designed.

Controlled degradation also protects the user's perception. A technical error without an
alternative turns an external dependency into a total failure of the experience. A secondary
path allows preserving continuity and clearly expressing which component is unavailable.

This principle comes from Industrial Engineering and from systems design. Not every failure can
be avoided, but its effects can be anticipated, contained and communicated. A resilient
architecture is not the one that denies the possibility of failing, but the one that decides in
advance how it will preserve its essential function when it happens.

## Cost is an architecture variable

<!-- seccion: costo-y-presupuesto -->

The chat operates under an explicit budget. The goal is not to show that a generative application
can run on the most expensive model or the widest context, but to sustain a public capability
whose cost stays proportional to the value it offers.

The target monthly budget is a ceiling of US$20 and the observed real cost is US$0, because usage
fits within the initial provider's free quota and because off-topic queries are resolved without
invoking it. This figure must be read within the real volume of use, the configured providers and
the current conditions of their services; it is not a promise of being free, it is a measurement.

The first saving decision happens before the model: out-of-scope questions consume no tokens. The
second is in retrieval: only the four relevant chunks are sent and not the entirety of the site's
content; with the cap of 180 words per chunk, the average context of an answer is about 540
words, on the order of 800 tokens. The third is in the history: the application keeps only the 12
messages needed to maintain coherence.

The size of the answers is also limited, to 600 tokens. A longer answer is not automatically more
useful and can increase cost, latency and the chance of introducing unnecessary claims. The
architecture seeks to produce a sufficient explanation, backed by the right sources.

Provider selection can also respond to cost, availability and capability. However, a cheaper
alternative is only valid if it preserves the minimum criteria of grounding, citation and
behavior the application demands.

Economy is not measured exclusively by the cost of one call. An incorrect answer that needs
several corrections, a deficient retrieval that forces rephrasing the question or a fragile
architecture that demands frequent maintenance also generate costs. AI-300 strengthens precisely
this operational view: taking artificial intelligence to production requires observing not only
the quality of the answer, but also the latency, consumption, availability and sustainability of
the solution throughout its life cycle.

## Security, abuse and operational limits

<!-- seccion: seguridad-y-limites -->

A public application must assume that not every request will be legitimate or aimed at the
purpose it was designed for. That is why the chat includes limits on the frequency of queries,
their length, the extent of the answers and the history kept, and all of them are written as
numbers, not as intentions: 10 questions per minute, 800 characters, 600 output tokens, 30
seconds, 12 messages.

Limiting per visitor and per period helps contain abusive automation, accidental consumption and
disproportionate use of capacity. It does not remove every possibility of abuse, but it reduces
exposure and provides a first barrier proportional to the application's reach.

The history is kept deliberately short. This limits the growth of the context, reduces cost and
lowers the chance that old instructions unpredictably alter a later answer. The application keeps
enough continuity without treating the entire conversation as permanently necessary information.

There is also a deactivation mechanism through configuration. Facing an operational problem, a
provider change or a security condition, the generative capability can be suspended without
taking down the rest of the site, and the button disappears with it.

The server composes the fundamental instructions and controls the credentials needed to
communicate with the providers. The keys live in the deployment's environment variables, are not
exposed to the browser and are not part of the public client code, and a gitleaks hook blocks any
commit that tries to sneak them into the repository.

These measures do not make the system invulnerable. They represent controls proportional to a
public application of delimited scope. Security is designed as a combination of prevention,
containment, visibility and capacity to respond.

Responsible publishing also demands not indiscriminately logging the content of conversations.
Telemetry is limited to what is necessary to understand the operation, investigate failures and
manage costs —provider, model, milliseconds and tokens— and never stores the question or the
answer: zero persisted answers, by design and by test.

## How I evaluate the RAG

<!-- seccion: evaluacion-del-rag -->

A RAG architecture should not be evaluated only by looking at a few convincing answers. It needs
a set of representative questions that allows analyzing retrieval, generation, citations,
out-of-scope rejection and degradation. Here two sets run on every change, with the same search
module production uses.

The first is the golden set: each document of this base declares in its header the three
questions it must answer, and the test requires that each question bring its document among the
first four results. The test travels with the content: adding a document adds its questions to
the gate without anyone editing the tests. With 25 documents that is 75 questions.

The second is the bank of questions written from outside, the way a recruiter or interviewer
asks: 136 legitimate questions in ten families —career, way of working, certifications, AI and
agents, platform and data, BI and analytics, governance, processes, showcase and fit—, plus 15
off-topic ones and 3 declared without coverage. Each legitimate one must bring an expected source
in the top-4, the expected source must arrive first in at least 60% of the cases, and the 15
off-topic ones must behave exactly as declared: which ones are blocked without spending a token
and which ones pass to the model, with their reason written beside them.

The questions cover direct facts, relationships between sections, indirect phrasings, typing
errors, ambiguous queries and requests that do not belong to the domain. They also include
questions whose answer does not exist, because the ability to declare a gap is an essential part
of correct behavior.

For generation, I verify that each factual claim is supported, that no data foreign to the corpus
is introduced and that the answer keeps the requested scope. For citations, I check that each
reference leads to a valid destination. And I evaluate behavior under failure: the system must
activate the local search when the provider is unavailable, communicate the degradation and keep
access to the relevant content. Costs and latency are part of the evaluation: a correct but
needlessly expensive or slow answer indicates too much context, excessive history or a
disproportionate configuration.

## The two measurements of the top-k and the vocabulary audit

<!-- seccion: medicion-del-top-k -->

How many sources enter an answer is not a matter of taste. The 4 comes from measuring, and the
two independent sets measure it against the complete index. The first measurement was in sprint
8, on 159 chunks, with 48 questions of my own and 131 from outside. The second, on September 20,
2026, on the 494 chunks, with 75 and 136:

| Sources per answer | Golden set (75 of my own) | Bank (136 from outside) | Average context |
| ------------------ | ------------------------: | ----------------------: | --------------: |
| 1                  |                     44/75 |                  98/136 |       136 words |
| 2                  |                     59/75 |                 120/136 |       267 words |
| 3                  |                     67/75 |                 130/136 |       402 words |
| **4**              |                 **75/75** |             **136/136** |       537 words |
| 5                  |                     75/75 |                 136/136 |       670 words |

With three sources, six of the 136 outside questions bring none of their own. With four none
fails, and going up to five rescues no one: it only enlarges the context by 25%, and the bill
with it. That two sets written with different criteria land on the same number, twice, is the
part that gives confidence. With a single source the golden set would hit 59%; today the number
lives in one place in the code and the golden set exercises it directly.

The bank is also a vocabulary audit, and that is its most valuable function. Since the search is
lexical, a word the corpus does not say does not exist for whoever asks. When the bank discovered
that "ETL" and "lakehouse" did not appear in the documents —I had written "information
integration"—, the content was corrected, not the expectation. A mandatory lexicon per document,
derived from the bank and from the published skills, now watches that those words stay there.

This discipline turns the RAG into an evaluable system and not a demo based on handpicked
examples. AI-103 provides the patterns for building generative and retrieval applications; AI-300
extends the capacity to evaluate, observe and operate those solutions in a sustained way. And a
control only acquires value when it can show that it detects a deviation: the tests include
provoked failures —citations to nonexistent destinations, out-of-domain queries, missing chunks,
a downed provider, answers that try to introduce information that was not retrieved— and every
new gate was seen in red before being accepted.

## What this application demonstrates about generative artificial intelligence

<!-- seccion: que-demuestra -->

The chat demonstrates that I can design a generative artificial intelligence solution as a
complete system and not only as a call to a model.

The architecture begins in the versioned content, continues in an index built and validated
beforehand, retrieves evidence through a mechanism proportional to the problem, delimits the
scope before consuming tokens and uses the model as an interchangeable synthesis layer among five
providers.

It also demonstrates that I can consciously decide what not to use. I did not bring in embeddings
only because they are customary in RAG architectures. I first implemented a lexical retrieval
that is explainable, reproducible and sufficient for the current corpus, and left the semantic
evolution conditional on evidence that it would produce a real improvement.

The system keeps traceability from the claim to the source. Navigable citations turn
verifiability into part of the experience and force the content, the index and the interface to
keep a consistent relationship.

The solution also demonstrates resilience. The generative dependency can fail without removing
access to the knowledge. The user receives a degraded but useful function, together with an
honest explanation of the condition.

The budget and the operational limits show that cost is part of the design. The architecture
filters questions before calling the model, restricts context and history, limits answers and
allows changing provider without rebuilding the entire application.

DP-600 is reflected in the preparation, structuring and governance of knowledge as an analytical
asset. AI-103 is reflected in building a generative application with retrieval, providers and
guardrails. AI-300 is reflected in the concern for evaluation, observability, resilience, costs
and operation.

Industrial Engineering contributes the process view: inputs, transformations, controls,
exceptions, outputs and feedback. Industrial Design contributes the experience through which the
user can understand the answer, walk through its evidence and recognize when the capability has
degraded.

The difference between declaring experience in RAG and demonstrating it is this application. I do
not need to limit myself to describing a possible architecture. With 75 questions of my own and
136 from outside running on every change, the visitor can ask a question, observe the answer,
open its sources and directly examine the decisions that sustain the system.
